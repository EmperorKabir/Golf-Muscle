## RECOMMENDATION (final)

### Verdict on the owner's opacity-only proposal: REJECTED as the primary/sole channel. Opacity is retained, but demoted to a bounded secondary/gating role.

Reasoning chain:
1. Opacity/transparency does not appear at all in the field's canonical channel-effectiveness rankings (Cleveland & McGill 1984, Munzner 2014 — S1, S2). It has never been validated as a magnitude channel by the reference literature visualization design is built on.
2. Where opacity magnitude-reading HAS been directly studied (S3, S4): it carries only a coarse directional bias ("more opaque reads as more"), is confounded by an independent, competing saturation bias, and its physical-to-perceived mapping is logarithmic and background/contrast-dependent — not the stable, near-linear, context-independent mapping a primary quantitative channel needs.
3. **Decisive problem (S5): opacity compositing is mathematically ambiguous under stacking, and stacking is this design's default condition, not an edge case.** By the standard "over" operator (Porter & Duff 1984), two same-colour zones at 50% opacity stacked composite to EXACTLY the same pixel as one zone at 75% (αo = αa + αb(1−αa) = 0.75). With ~240 zones and routine overlap along any view ray through a see-through rotating figure, a viewer looking at a patch of "75%-looking" colour cannot tell whether they are seeing one very active muscle or two moderately active ones stacked — the exact ambiguity the brief was worried about is real, exact, and unavoidable by the mathematics of alpha blending itself, not a rendering-quality issue that better engineering fixes. Domain-matched precedent (Diepstraten et al. 2002, technical/anatomical illustration with overlapping transparent layers, S5) reached the same conclusion independently and had to abandon fixed per-object opacity for depth/importance-dependent transparency — which fixes legibility, not the value-reading problem.
4. Practically, the real-time technique needed to afford ~240 overlapping alpha zones on a continuously-rotating figure without an exact (and expensive: ~4x frame cost, ~200–800 MB extra memory, S10) per-frame depth sort is an *approximate* compositing scheme (weighted blended OIT) that does not even reproduce the exact alpha values being asked to encode data — so the rendering technique required for performance further corrupts opacity-as-data on top of the unavoidable stacking ambiguity above it.
5. This is consistent with, and extends, the project's own prior finding (F-005 / `docs/research/06-activation-curve-and-colour-method.md`): a flat-hue, opacity-only red ramp was already flagged as failing CVD accessibility (8% of men) and mid-range discrimination, with a monotonic luminance ramp recommended instead. That prior analysis addressed the colour-only sub-problem; this investigation adds the stacking/compositing argument, which is independently sufficient to reject opacity-only even before the accessibility argument is considered.

### Recommended encoding

- **Primary channel — monotonic luminance/lightness ramp** (single warm hue family travelling dark→bright, e.g. the project's own prototyped `rampWarm`, study 10 in `model-style-studies.html`, in the spirit of viridis/magma/inferno's monotonic-lightness construction). This is what actually carries "how much is this muscle working" and is legible regardless of what is or is not composited behind it, because colour hue/lightness at a surface does not change identity when other transparent surfaces sit behind it the way opacity's apparent value does.
- **Secondary, REDUNDANT channel — bounded opacity, ceiling-capped, agreeing with luminance.** Let alpha rise with activation but clamp it to a modest ceiling (e.g. ~0.85, never near-total zone-on-zone saturation) so it contributes (a) the "quiet muscles genuinely fade toward invisible" feel the owner wants, and (b) a redundancy bonus consistent with S8's measured 66%→88% accuracy gain and 7–10% speed gain from combining channels — provided the two channels are yoked to the same value and never allowed to diverge (S3's lesson: independent, competing channels degrade each other; redundant, agreeing channels reinforce). Never use opacity as the ONLY place the number lives.
- **Depth-cueing use of opacity is separate and fine to keep** — study 03's distance-fade (fainter/thinner for zones further from camera) is not data encoding, it is a depth-perception aid, and should be layered independently on top of the luminance+bounded-alpha pair, with a floor so a genuinely hot far-side muscle never fades to invisible.
- **Threshold-gated emissive/glow rim** (already prototyped, studies 02/07/09: "rim" + "glow" filter) for zones above a activation floor. Recommended for two independent reasons: perceptually it separates adjacent hot muscles that would otherwise merge (S8's grouping/segmentation finding), and technically additive/emissive blending is commutative and needs no depth sort (S10), so it is close to free to add on top of whatever transparency technique is used for the shell.
- **Rendering**: do not attempt exact per-frame depth-sorted OIT across all ~240 zones — the ~4x cost and hundreds of MB of memory (S10) buys correctness for a channel (opacity) that is not the primary data carrier under this recommendation, so the expense is not justified. Approximate/weighted blended OIT (or simple back-to-front sort with a reasonable zone budget) is adequate for the shell's see-through aesthetic, precisely because the actual data readout (luminance) does not depend on compositing correctness.

### "No data" and "uncertain" states (extends the project's existing tier A/B/C/D system, `docs/design/muscle-zone-list.md`)

- **No data (tier D, ~2/3 of zones)**: outline only, always drawn (already established project rule — "keeps tier D zones honestly visible"), NO fill at all — not even the ramp's zero-value colour, because a measured-zero and a never-measured zone must be visually distinct per the brief. Recommend a quieter stroke treatment than measured zones (lower stroke-alpha and/or a cool/neutral tint rather than the warm family used for real data) so it reads as "structurally present, not yet lit up" rather than "confirmed zero" — quiet, but discoverable on inspection (tap/hover reveals "not measured").
- **Uncertain/provisional (tiers B/C)**: apply the VSUP mechanism (S9) — compress the usable range of the luminance ramp for lower-confidence tiers so an uncertain zone can never render at the ramp's most saturated/confident-looking extreme even if its point estimate is high; this stops viewers over-reading precision from colour intensity alone (the same failure mode as error bars, S9). Pair with a qualitatively different marker — light, sparse hatching or a desaturated variant of the same hue family — reserved only for this minority tier, so cost/clutter objections raised against hatching-as-a-universal-channel (style-study 05) do not apply. The distinguishing signal must be a difference in *kind*, not just a paler point on the same ramp, or it will read as "less active" rather than "less certain."

### Top risks
1. **Designers/engineers regress to a single opacity value out of implementation convenience** once "opacity fades unused muscles" is approved — the spec must make the ceiling-cap and luminance-primary rule explicit and testable (e.g. a code-level assertion that fill colour lightness, not just alpha, changes with activation), or the rejected design creeps back in through the back door.
2. **Depth-cue fade and data-driven alpha both target the same visual property (opacity)** and can interact unpredictably (a far-side, highly-active muscle could still look muted) unless the two are composed with an explicit floor/precedence rule and tested on real overlapping geometry, not just the flat 2D style-study mockups.
3. **Tier boundaries (A/B/C/D) are a categorical confidence system, but VSUP-style range compression needs a continuous or graded uncertainty measure to look intentional rather than banded/stepped** — an uncertain zone whose luminance range suddenly snaps at a tier boundary can look like a rendering bug; needs either more confidence granularity or a deliberately soft transition at tier edges.

### S1 — Cleveland & McGill (1984) elementary perceptual tasks, ranked most->least accurate
1. Position along a common (aligned) scale
2. Position along non-aligned scales
3. Length / direction / angle (grouped, roughly tied)
4. Area
5. Volume, curvature
6. Shading / colour saturation — LEAST accurate
Basis: psychophysical judgement experiments; theoretically grounded in Stevens' Power Law — length has exponent ~1 (accurate), area <1 (systematic underestimation), volume much lower (severe underestimation). Replicated by Heer & Bostock (2010, crowdsourced) with same ordering.
Source: Cleveland & McGill 1984, "Graphical Perception: Theory, Experimentation, and Application" (JASA); secondary summary via textbookofusability.com/glossary/cleveland-mcgill-hierarchy.html and vizdata.org STA313 slides (Duke).
Note: opacity/transparency/alpha is NOT part of the original 1984 task set at all — it predates alpha-blended computer graphics as a common encoding.

### S3 — Schoenlein, Sidibe & Schloss (2026), "Understanding the opaque-is-more bias and saturated-is-more bias for colormap data visualizations", Attention, Perception & Psychophysics 88(3), DOI 10.3758/s13414-025-03172-w, PMC12929228 (OA)
- Confirms an "opaque-is-more bias": viewers DO intuitively read "more opaque = larger quantity" — but this was measured as a forced-choice DIRECTIONAL judgement ("which side looks like more"), not fine-grained magnitude estimation of a specific value.
- Effect sizes (logistic regression, forced-choice opaque-side selection above chance): Exp.1 saturated-on-desaturated β=2.663, SE=.597, z=4.46, p<.001, OR=14.33; gray-on-saturated β=.724, SE=.305, z=2.376, p=.018, OR=2.06. Exp.2 replication: saturated β=3.045, p<.001, OR=21; desaturated β=.659, p=.038, OR=1.93. Exp.3 (hue-only, constant sat/lightness): blue bg β=8.657, p<.001; green bg β=−7.227, p=.004. Exp.4 (saturated-is-more isolation): β=3.974, p=.022, OR=53.2.
- Discovered a NEW, competing "saturated-is-more bias": more saturated also reads as "more", and when opacity and saturation cues conflict, the saturation bias measurably reduces (but does not eliminate) the opacity-driven judgement — i.e. two channels that both look like plausible magnitude cues can fight each other and degrade the readout.
- Authors' own recommendation: "we do not advocate for using these color scales in practice because lightness variation is important for revealing patterns of spatial variation in colormap data visualizations" — i.e. even the authors of the paper most sympathetic to opacity-as-data steer designers back to luminance/lightness ramps for real encoding work, reserving opacity-driven judgements as a studied bias rather than a recommended primary channel.
- Cites Roth et al. (2010) "value-by-alpha maps" as prior art for opacity-as-magnitude; cites Ekroll et al. 2004 (translucency perceivable from isoluminant 2D stimuli) vs Fleming & Bülthoff 2005 / Motoyoshi 2010 (lightness critical for translucency perception in 3D forms) — relevant because our figure is a 3D form, where the literature leans toward lightness/luminance mattering more than pure alpha.
- Relevance to Q1: shows opacity communicates ORDINAL direction reasonably (people know "more opaque-ish" = more), but the study never tests precise magnitude recovery (e.g. "is this 40% or 60% activation?"), and flags that opacity judgements are contaminated by a second, independently-acting bias (saturation) — exactly the kind of confound that undermines opacity as a clean, isolated quantitative channel.

### S4 — Aguilar & Maertens (2022), "Conjoint measurement of perceived transparency and perceived contrast" (maximum likelihood conjoint measurement study)
- Directly measured how perceived transparency relates to physical transmittance/reflectance of an overlay.
- **Finding: perceived transparency is a logarithmic (not linear) function of physical transmittance** — same family of contrast metric (log Michelson/Whittle contrast) that predicts perceived contrast. I.e. equal physical steps in alpha do NOT produce equal perceived steps — compression at high transmittance, expansion at low, mirroring the well-known Stevens'-law-type nonlinearities already established for area/volume in Cleveland & McGill's hierarchy (S1).
- Relevance: even setting aside the overlap/stacking problem (S5 below), opacity behaves like a *low-ranked* magnitude channel because its physical-to-perceptual mapping is nonlinear and contrast-dependent (perceived transparency of an overlay changes with what's behind it) — the opposite of what a reliable quantitative channel needs (a monotonic, background-independent, roughly linear percept).

### S5 — Alpha-compositing mathematics & order-dependence (foundational CG + technical-illustration literature)
- **Porter & Duff (1984), "Compositing Digital Images," SIGGRAPH '84 Proceedings, 18:253–259** — defines the "over" operator: compositing layer A (front, alpha αa) over layer B (back, alpha αb) yields output alpha **αo = αa + αb(1 − αa)**.
  - Worked example for the design brief's own scenario: two same-colour zones at 50% opacity stacked (front αa=0.5, back αb=0.5): αo = 0.5 + 0.5×(1−0.5) = **0.75** — i.e. mathematically and exactly identical to a single zone rendered at 75%. This is not a rare edge case; it is the deterministic, universal behaviour of the standard "over" compositing operator that all real-time renderers use for alpha blending. **Two muscles at half-intensity stacked are literally indistinguishable in the final pixel from one muscle at three-quarter intensity.** With ~240 zones and multiple zones routinely stacking along a view ray in a transparent figure, this ambiguity is not an edge case — it is the default operating condition of the whole design.
- **Order dependence**: alpha blending is not commutative/associative in general — "for a correct result, surfaces must be blended from farthest to nearest or nearest to farthest" (standard graphics-pipeline requirement; see Wikipedia "Order-independent transparency" summary of the standard pipeline, and Everitt 2001 depth-peeling, and McGuire & Bavoil 2013 weighted-blended OIT, JCGT vol.2 no.2). Sorting "can take a significant amount of time, not always produce a solution (in the case of intersecting or circularly overlapping geometry), and the implementation is complex." A rotating figure means the correct sort order changes every frame for all ~240 zones.
- **Weighted Blended OIT (McGuire & Bavoil 2013)**, the standard real-time approximate solution that avoids per-frame sorting, works by replacing exact compositing with a *commutative approximation weighted by depth* — meaning it deliberately does NOT reproduce the exact Porter-Duff result. Consequence for this project: even the practical real-time technique that avoids the sorting cost problem does so by further distorting the numeric relationship between a zone's "true" alpha value and its rendered appearance. Opacity-as-data would be corrupted twice over: once by unavoidable compositing math when stacked (exact, S5a), and again by the approximation error of whatever OIT technique is used to keep frame rate acceptable (S8 below).
- **Diepstraten, Weiskopf & Ertl (2002), "Transparency in Interactive Technical Illustrations," Computer Graphics Forum 21(3)** (Eurographics) — the closest prior-art match to this project's exact scenario (interactive 3D technical/anatomical illustration with multiple overlapping transparent surfaces). Central finding: **fixed per-object opacity fails once objects overlap** — stacked transparent layers produce ambiguous, hard-to-interpret images, which is why the paper introduces depth-dependent and importance-driven transparency (opacity computed dynamically per-pixel from depth/context, not read directly off a stored per-object value) rather than a single static alpha per surface. This is a domain-matched precedent (illustrative anatomy, not just abstract dataviz) independently reaching the same conclusion the brief worries about: static per-zone opacity does not survive overlap in a see-through 3D figure.

### S6 — Crameri, Shephard & Heron (2020), "The misuse of colour in science communication", Nature Communications 11:5444, DOI 10.1038/s41467-020-19160-7, PMC7595127 (OA)
- Core principle (quoted): a given data step "should appear the same no matter whether it occurs at low [values] ... or high [values]" — i.e. the colour ramp must be perceptually uniform/monotonic in lightness across its whole range, not just at the extremes.
- **Numeric finding: perceptually non-uniform colour maps introduce visual error of >7% of the displayed data variation** — a directly quotable distortion figure caused purely by uneven lightness gradients (e.g. rainbow/jet maps), even before any transparency/overlap problem is added.
- **Colour-vision deficiency (CVD) prevalence, precise: 0.5% of women and 8% of men** are affected worldwide (higher in populations with more white/Caucasian ancestry, e.g. Scandinavia). "Colour maps that include both red and green colours with similar lightness cannot be read by a large fraction of the readership" — rainbow maps in particular "fail to reproduce a meaningful smooth gradient" under CVD.
- Recommended maps: viridis, magma, plasma, inferno (Matplotlib); batlow, oslo, cividis (Crameri's "Scientific colour maps"); CMOcean.
- Recommendation: choose colour-map class by data type (sequential for magnitude, diverging for signed deviation, cyclic for phase, multi-sequential for multivariate) via a decision tree; always show a colour bar/legend; never modify a perceptually-uniform map after the fact (e.g. do not brighten/darken parts of it, which reintroduces non-uniformity).
- Direct relevance: this is the single strongest evidence for using a monotonic-lightness ramp (dark→light or vice-versa, single hue such as the project's red family or a purpose-built sequential ramp) as the PRIMARY channel for activation magnitude, because (a) uniform lightness steps read as uniform data steps (no >7% distortion), and (b) lightness survives red-green CVD (present in ~8% of men) whereas an iso-luminant red-green or pure-saturation ramp does not.

### S7 — Liu & Heer (2018), "Somewhere Over the Rainbow: An Empirical Assessment of Quantitative Colormaps", CHI 2018 (ACM), DOI 10.1145/3173574.3174172
- Confirmed to exist at this venue/DOI (CHI '18, "Perception in Visualization" session); paywalled at ACM DL and ResearchGate for this pass (403/402 responses), and the PDF mirror at idl.cs.washington.edu could not be text-extracted through available tooling (encoded PDF stream) — exact error percentages from this paper could NOT be independently re-verified this session and are omitted rather than guessed, per the no-guessing rule.
- What is confirmed: this is a controlled empirical study (crowdsourced quantitative-reading tasks) comparing rainbow/jet-type colormaps against monotonic, perceptually-ordered colormaps (viridis-family), consistent in direction with Crameri et al. (S6) — both papers argue from empirical/theoretical grounds that non-monotonic or hue-varying colour ramps degrade quantitative reading accuracy relative to monotonic-lightness ramps. Treated here as corroborating, not load-bearing on its own; S6's numbers are the ones relied on for the recommendation.

### S8 — Nothelfer, Gleicher & Franconeri (2017), "Redundant Encoding Strengthens Segmentation and Grouping in Visual Displays of Data," J. Exp. Psychol.: Human Perception & Performance, and companion 2017/2018 "Redundant Encoding in Data Visualizations: Assessing Perceptual Accuracy and Speed" (Information Visualization journal)
- Direct empirical test of redundant (multi-channel) vs single-channel encoding.
- **Accuracy: redundantly encoding the same data by BOTH colour and shape gave 88% accuracy (mean across 3 experiments), vs 66% for colour alone and 58% for shape alone.**
- **Speed: participants were 7–10% faster** detecting the global structure of a collection of redundantly-encoded objects than with either channel alone; this generalised to a more realistic "find the sparsest quadrant" task.
- Grouping/segmentation: "grouping is much stronger when objects are redundantly encoded by luminance & shape than when encoded by either dimension alone" — explicitly includes luminance as one of the tested redundant channels.
- Source (summary page, author's own site): cnothelfer.com/redundant-encoding, cross-referenced against graphics.cs.wisc.edu/Papers/2015/NGF15/ngf15.pdf and graphics.cs.wisc.edu/Papers/2017/NGF17 (PDF text-extraction failed via WebFetch tooling; numbers taken from the author's own HTML summary of the same study, cross-referenced against the arXiv/Tandfonline listing found independently).
- Direct relevance to Q4: this is controlled, numbers-backed evidence that combining channels beats a single channel for both accuracy and speed — i.e. redundant encoding (luminance ramp + a secondary channel such as glow/rim-brightening or a modest opacity floor-to-ceiling range) should out-perform any single channel used alone, PROVIDED the redundant channels agree with each other rather than compete (contrast with S3's finding that opacity and saturation can conflict and degrade readout when they disagree — redundancy helps only when channels are yoked to the same underlying value, not left free to diverge).

### S9 — Uncertainty visualisation literature
- **Correll, Moritz & Heer (2018), "Value-Suppressing Uncertainty Palettes," CHI 2018**, DOI 10.1145/3173574.3174216 (CMU / UW). Mechanism: a VSUP is a bivariate (value × uncertainty) colour palette that **deliberately shrinks the usable range of the value channel as uncertainty rises** — "VSUPs allocate larger ranges of a visual channel to data when uncertainty is low, and smaller ranges when uncertainty is high," which (a) uses the limited channel more economically when confidence is high and (b) forces more cautious reading when confidence is low. Crowdsourced evaluation found VSUPs, compared to conventional bivariate (value+uncertainty) maps, **make people weight the uncertainty information more heavily in decisions** — i.e. it is not just decorative, it measurably changes downstream judgement in the intended direction. Sources: idl.uw.edu/vsup/, dig.cmu.edu/publications/2018-vsup.html.
- **Correll & Gleicher (2014), "Error Bars Considered Harmful: Exploring Alternate Encodings for Mean and Standard Deviation," IEEE TVCG 20(12):2142–2151**, DOI 10.1109/TVCG.2014.2346298. Finding: standard error bars systematically mislead viewers about effect size and distribution — participants conflate SE vs CI, misjudge overlap significance, and underestimate true variability; gradient/blur-based uncertainty encodings performed comparably or better than error bars, and violin-plot-style distributional encodings outperformed both for conveying actual shape rather than a bare point-plus-interval. Relevance: a hard cut-off ("uncertain" vs "confident" as a binary badge) is the encoding equivalent of an error bar's false precision — better to fade/desaturate/suppress range continuously with confidence tier than to use one flat "warning" symbol.
- **Kinkeldey (systematic review), "How to Assess Visual Communication of Uncertainty? A Systematic Review of Geospatial Uncertainty Visualisation User Studies"** — establishes that most uncertainty-visualisation research is not empirically evaluated against real user tasks, and argues the field should move to task-centred typologies. Numeric breakdown of technique-by-technique effectiveness could not be extracted from available sources this session (review PDF not text-extractable via WebFetch tooling); cited here only for its general, well-supported conclusion — task fit matters more than any single "correct" uncertainty channel — used to justify tailoring the no-data/uncertain treatment to this project's specific task (browsing/comparing muscles at a glance) rather than importing a generic scheme unmodified.
- Broader uncertainty-visualisation convention (general knowledge, corroborated by the above): texture/hatching and desaturation are the two most common "this value is not fully trustworthy" encodings precisely because they are visually distinct from both the "on" state (saturated colour) and the "off" state (nothing) — they read as a third, qualitatively different appearance rather than a point partway along the same ramp. The project's own style-study 05 (fibre-direction hatching, `docs/design/model-style-studies.html` lines 634–645) already prototyped hatching and rejected it *as the primary effort indicator* on cost/clutter grounds ("costs a lot in 3D... clutters at the intensity you most need to read") — but that objection applies to hatching-as-the-main-channel-for-all-240-zones-at-all-times. Used sparingly, only on the minority of zones sitting in tiers B/C, hatching/texture does not carry that cost, and is exactly the kind of qualitatively-distinct marker the uncertainty-vis literature calls for.

### S10 — Practical real-time rendering: order-independent transparency and emissive/glow blending
- Standard alpha "over" compositing is **order-dependent by construction**: the correct pixel colour is a weighted sum in which each layer's contribution is scaled by the *product of all transmittances of layers in front of it* — collapse that to a naive unsorted blend and the result is wrong, not just imprecise (interplayoflight.wordpress.com technical breakdown of the compositing equation, cross-checked against the Porter & Duff 1984 "over" formula in S5).
- **Real measured cost of doing OIT exactly** (per-pixel linked list / A-buffer technique, full correctness): **~5.52 ms/frame** (4.20 ms fragment-listing + 1.32 ms sort-and-blend) vs **~1.37 ms/frame** for standard hardware alpha blending in the same scene — roughly a **4x** per-frame cost increase for guaranteed-correct transparency ordering, plus **~200 MB of extra memory at 1080p for 8 fragments/pixel (≈800 MB at 4K)**. Source: interplayoflight.wordpress.com/2022/06/25/order-independent-transparency-part-1/ (rendering-engineer technical blog, numbers from the author's own benchmarked demo scene — treated as an order-of-magnitude engineering data point, not a peer-reviewed figure, but directly on-point for the "is per-frame depth-sorting affordable" question).
- **Approximate OIT (weighted blended, McGuire & Bavoil 2013, JCGT 2(2))** avoids the sort/memory cost by using a *commutative, depth-weighted approximation* instead of exact back-to-front compositing — cheap and order-independent, but by construction does not reproduce exact Porter-Duff alpha values. For this project that means: even the affordable real-time technique for handling ~240 overlapping zones on a rotating figure does not preserve exact per-zone opacity-as-data: it approximates.
- **Additive/emissive blending is commutative and does not require depth sorting** — mathematically, setting a fragment's transmittance contribution such that layers simply sum (rather than being weighted by the product of preceding transmittances) removes the order-dependence entirely: `sum(colour_i)` does not depend on the order the terms are summed in. This is why glow/bloom/emissive effects in real-time engines are conventionally additive rather than alpha-blended. **However, additive blending does not solve the magnitude-reading problem either** — it swaps one ambiguity for another: two zones glowing at 50% additively summed are visually similar to (though not identical to, given tone-mapping/clamping non-linearities) one zone at 100%, so stacking ambiguity is reduced in severity (no asymptotic compression toward white the way repeated alpha-over compresses toward opaque) but not eliminated. It is therefore a genuinely better *rendering-cost* answer than exact per-frame depth-sorted alpha blending, but not, on its own, a fix for "can I read the exact value of one zone when others overlap it" — that requires the primary readout to not depend on compositing at all (see recommendation).

### S2 — Munzner (Visualization Analysis & Design, 2014) channel effectiveness ranking
Magnitude channels (ordered attributes), most -> least effective:
1. Position on common scale
2. Position on unaligned scale
3. Length (1D size)
4. Tilt/angle
5. Area (2D size)
6. Depth (3D position)
7. Color luminance
8. Color saturation
9. Curvature
10. Volume (3D size)

Identity channels (categorical, not for magnitude): Spatial region, Color hue, Motion, Shape.
Source: T. Munzner, UBC CS547 course slides "Marks and Channels" (cs.ubc.ca/~tmm/courses/547-20/slides/marks.pdf — PDF text-extraction failed via WebFetch, confirmed instead via SlideShare mirror of her Lecture 2 deck, "Channels: Expressiveness types and effectiveness rankings").
**Key finding: opacity/transparency does not appear anywhere in Munzner's canonical magnitude- or identity-channel list.** It is absent from the taxonomy used as the field's standard reference for channel effectiveness — i.e. it has not been empirically validated/ranked as a magnitude channel in the primary perceptual-ranking literature at all. This is itself evidence against treating opacity as a first-class quantitative channel: the visualization community's own effectiveness ranking has no slot for it.
