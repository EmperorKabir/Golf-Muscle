## 1. Intra-abdominal pressure (IAP) as a spinal stabilising mechanism

**Mechanism.** The abdominal cavity, bounded superiorly by the diaphragm, inferiorly by the pelvic
floor, and circumferentially by transversus abdominis (TrA) and the abdominal wall, behaves as a
pressurised fluid-filled cylinder. Coordinated contraction of these four muscle groups raises IAP,
which (a) converts the abdomen into a semi-rigid strut that unloads the spine via a direct extensor
moment, and (b) increases the passive/active stiffness of the trunk as a whole, resisting buckling
under compressive and shear load [source 15, 57].

**Direct causal evidence (pressure → stiffness, isolated from voluntary muscle activity).**
Hodges et al. 2005 used **phrenic nerve stimulation** to raise IAP involuntarily (bypassing
voluntary trunk muscle contraction) in three subjects: IAP rose by **27–61%**, and lumbar spine
stiffness increased by **8–31%** as a direct consequence — the first mechanistic isolation of
pressure-only stiffening from muscle-only stiffening [1]. A companion study in side-lying subjects
found that phrenic-stimulation-driven IAP increases (again with no abdominal muscle activity)
produced a **trunk extensor moment of approximately 6 Nm** — the first in vivo quantification of
the moment IAP alone contributes [57].

**Voluntary IAP and trunk stiffness (Cholewicki/McGill programme).**
- Theoretical spine-stability model: critical buckling load increased with either antagonistic
  muscle coactivation force or IAP; the model matched experimental data to within 95.5%
  average error [7].
- At **40% and 80% of maximal voluntary IAP**: trunk stiffness increased **21% and 42%** in
  flexion, and **16% and 30%** in lateral bending, respectively. An external abdominal belt added a
  further **9–57%** stiffness gain depending on direction and IAP level [8].
- Spine stability and L4/L5 compression force increased **proportionally with IAP regardless of
  whether the IAP rise was voluntary or the subject consciously tried to avoid raising it** —
  i.e. IAP and trunk muscle co-contraction cannot be fully decoupled even when a subject tries
  [9].
- A lumbosacral orthosis (LSO) increased effective trunk stiffness by **160 Nm/rad (27%)** across
  all directions tested [11].

**Numeric IAP values under real physical load (not modelled, directly measured via
intragastric/rectal catheter or comparable transducer).**
- **Weightlifting/jumping** (Harman et al. 1988, classic study): peak IAP during a maximal
  Valsalva manoeuvre averaged **26.6 ± 6.7 kPa (~200 mmHg)**, with one individual reaching
  **36.9 kPa (277 mmHg)**. Across tasks, peak IAP ranked (ascending): bench press < vertical jump
  < drop jump < box lift < deadlift < leg press < slide row < Valsalva [51].
- **Resistance-training systematic review** (Blazek et al. 2019): squats generated IAP **>200 mmHg**;
  deadlift, sled row and leg press **161–176 mmHg**; bench press **79 ± 44 mmHg** [50].
- **Biomechanical modelling** (Stokes et al. 2010): IAP of **5 or 10 kPa (37.5 or 75 mmHg)**
  produced measurable spinal-unloading effects across flexion, extension, lateral bending, *and
  rotation* tasks in the model [15].
- **General surgical-literature review** (Nygaard et al. 2013) states plainly that "the ranges of
  intra-abdominal pressures during specific activities are large" and that pressures during
  medically restricted versus unrestricted post-operative activities **overlap considerably** — a
  caution against assuming any single activity (including golf) has a fixed, predictable IAP
  signature [52].
- **Isokinetic lifting/lowering** (Cresswell & Thorstensson 1994): TrA EMG amplitude had the best
  correlation with IAP variation across contraction velocities (**r = 0.970**); IAP was *lower*
  during eccentric lowering than concentric lifting **despite greater force production** during
  lowering — IAP does not simply track force output [54].
- **Decoupling pressure from moment** (Cresswell & Thorstensson 1989): IAP can be raised without
  a large flexor counter-moment being generated — evidence that diaphragm and TrA can regulate
  pressure somewhat independently of the trunk-flexion moment that a rectus/oblique contraction
  would otherwise create [56].

**Numeric anchor for golf-scale forces:** no golf IAP figure exists (see §5). The lifting/jumping
figures above (**~80–280 mmHg** depending on task and effort) are the only quantitative anchor
available; a golf downswing is a much lower peak-force, higher-velocity rotational task, so these
values should be treated as an upper-bound context, not a golf estimate.

**Measurement/reporting methods across this literature:** intragastric pressure via swallowed or
naso-gastric balloon catheter (the standard technique in the Hodges/Cholewicki/Cresswell/
Thorstensson body of work) or, in critical-care literature, intravesical (bladder) catheter — see
§7 for full methodological detail.

## 2. The diaphragm as a postural/stabilising muscle — not merely respiratory

**Core finding:** the diaphragm has a dual role and both roles use the same motor units and nerve
(phrenic), which can conflict under load.

- Hodges, Butler, McKenzie & Gandevia 1997: during rapid shoulder flexion, diaphragm EMG activity
  began **~20 ms prior to the onset of deltoid EMG** — i.e. it fires in a feedforward, anticipatory
  pattern exactly like a limb-stabilising postural muscle, not in response to the movement. Onset
  of diaphragm EMG **coincided with the onset of TrA EMG** — the two co-activate as a single
  functional unit [4].
- Hodges & Gandevia 2000 (*J Appl Physiol*): during limb movements that challenge trunk stability,
  coactivation of diaphragm and abdominal muscles causes a **sustained increase in IAP**; opposing
  (antagonist) muscle activity is then used to regulate the smaller respiratory pressure
  oscillations that continue to ride on top of that raised baseline — i.e. the diaphragm keeps
  breathing *and* stabilises the trunk simultaneously, by pressure-modulation rather than by
  stopping one function for the other [2].
- Hodges & Gandevia 2000 (*J Physiol*), repetitive postural task: diaphragm was active throughout
  expiration at **78 ± 17% of its peak inspiratory EMG magnitude**, with modulation locked to both
  the respiratory rhythm and the movement-repetition frequency simultaneously — direct evidence of
  dual-task motor drive converging on the same muscle [3].
- **The conflict, when it is resolved in favour of breathing:** Hodges, Heijnen & Gandevia 2001
  raised respiratory drive experimentally (hypercapnoea, 60 s of elevated CO₂) and found postural
  diaphragm activity was **reduced or absent**, i.e. when ventilatory demand is high enough, the
  respiratory role of the diaphragm displaces its stabilising role at a **pre-motoneuronal**
  (central, not spinal-reflex) level [5]. This is the mechanistic basis for why breath-holding or
  altered breathing under exertion is plausible as a *deliberate* stability strategy — see §6.
- Gandevia, Butler, Hodges & Taylor 2002 (review): synthesises the above — challenges to posture
  can indirectly challenge ventilation and vice versa, because the same muscle (and to a lesser
  extent the abdominal wall) subserves both [6].
- **Ultrasound confirmation, non-EMG method:** Sembera et al. 2022 (M-mode ultrasonography) found
  the diaphragm's postural function is "independent of its respiratory activity" and is
  **unaffected by increased abdominal wall tension during load-lifting tasks** — i.e. it keeps
  contributing to stability even when the abdominal wall itself is already tensed for a different
  reason [13].
- Illidi & Romer 2022: diaphragm power output during lower-limb exercise is preserved via
  "coordinated changes in contractile shortening" — a mechanical adaptation that lets the muscle
  keep serving both roles under load [14].
- Mannion et al. 2008 (tissue Doppler / ultrasound feedforward method): the earliest measurable
  abdominal-wall feedforward activity occurred **<50 ms after the onset of medial deltoid EMG**
  using a non-EMG (Doppler tissue-velocity) detection method; the mean discrepancy between this
  ultrasound-based method and simultaneous EMG timing was **20 ms**, which is itself a useful
  bound on how precisely feedforward timing can be pinned down across methods [20]. (Contrast
  with the classical EMG literature, which places TrA/diaphragm onset *before*, not after, prime-
  mover EMG — the sign/magnitude discrepancy across methods and movement types is real and
  unresolved in these abstracts; flagged as a genuine literature inconsistency rather than
  reconciled here.)

**Conclusion for the model:** the diaphragm is not merely a respiratory bellows. Independent of the
breathing cycle, it fires as a genuine postural/trunk-stabilising muscle in a feedforward manner
essentially simultaneous with TrA, and this contribution is only withdrawn when ventilatory demand
becomes high enough to override it centrally.

## 3. Pelvic floor co-activation with the rest of the deep core

- **Sapsford et al. 2001** (the foundational co-activation paper): abdominal muscle activity (EO,
  TrA, RA) is "a normal response to pelvic floor exercise" in asymptomatic subjects — i.e. a
  maximal voluntary pelvic-floor contraction reliably recruits the abdominal wall as a synergist,
  not merely a bystander. TrA showed **greater** activation than rectus abdominis during pelvic
  floor contraction, and this relationship was modulated by spinal position (greater TrA response
  in spinal extension) [44].
- **Direction of coupling also runs pelvic-floor → respiration:** Park, Hwang & Kim 2015 found
  voluntary pelvic floor muscle (PFM) contraction promotes activation of the respiratory muscles
  and increases vital capacity — evidence the coupling is bidirectional, not just "abdominals
  recruit PFM" but also "PFM recruits respiratory muscles" [45].
- **Systematic review (Vesentini et al. 2019):** across studies, increased TrA cocontraction is
  associated with maximal PFM contraction in women *without* pelvic floor dysfunction (PFD); in
  women *with* PFD, cocontraction shifts toward internal/external oblique instead — i.e. the
  normal deep-core recruitment pattern (PFM co-activating preferentially with the deepest
  abdominal layer, TrA) degrades toward a more superficial substitution pattern under dysfunction,
  directly analogous to the TrA-delay finding in low back pain (§4) [22].
- Silva et al. 2016: when TrA/internal oblique MVC was solicited via a training protocol, PFM EMG
  activity increased simultaneously (**p = 0.001**) [23].
- Hwang & Park 2017: combining the abdominal draw-in manoeuvre with deliberate PFM co-activation
  produced a statistically significant shift in the TrA/internal-oblique preferential contraction
  ratio (**p < 0.05**) versus other irradiation strategies [25].
- **Multi-muscle ultrasound case-control (Cervera-Cano et al. 2024):** measured pelvic floor,
  lumbar multifidus, *and* diaphragm excursion together at rest and during multiple loading
  manoeuvres in people with and without low back pain, finding significant group differences in
  contraction indices — the closest thing in this literature to a unified "deep core as one
  system" measurement protocol, though again **not golf-specific** [24].

**No golf-specific pelvic floor study of any kind was located** (see §5).

## 4. Transversus abdominis feedforward activation, and the low back pain delay

**The core finding (qualitative, well replicated):** TrA activates *before* prime-mover EMG onset
in a task-direction-independent, feedforward (centrally pre-programmed, not reflexive) manner in
healthy people, and this feedforward timing is **disrupted (delayed) in people with low back
pain**.

- Hodges & Richardson 1996 (*Spine*, the original clinical paper): "the delayed onset of
  contraction of transversus abdominis indicates a deficit of motor control" hypothesised to cause
  inefficient muscular stabilisation of the spine; TrA was "invariably the first muscle active" and
  "not influenced by movement direction" in controls, while contraction "was significantly
  delayed... with all [three tested] movements" in LBP patients [21].
- Hodges & Richardson 1997 (*Exp Brain Res*, the mechanism paper): across shoulder flexion,
  abduction and extension, "the onset of TrA EMG occurred in advance of deltoid irrespective of
  movement direction," while every other trunk muscle tested (internal oblique, external oblique,
  rectus abdominis, lumbar multifidus) showed direction-*dependent* timing — TrA alone behaves as a
  generic, direction-agnostic spinal-stiffening reflex rather than a directional balance-correction
  muscle [59].
- Hodges & Richardson 1999 (*Neurosci Lett*): the timing of TrA's anticipatory contraction was
  **invariant** even while the subject's own limb-movement reaction time varied trial-to-trial —
  TrA is centrally locked to movement *preparation*, not to when the limb actually moves, and is
  controlled independently of the superficial abdominal muscles [17].
- Hodges, Butler, McKenzie & Gandevia 1997: **TrA onset coincided with diaphragm onset**, both
  **~20 ms before** deltoid EMG onset [4] — this is the most precise recoverable feedforward-timing
  number in this search (the classic Hodges/Richardson 1996 Spine and 1997 Exp Brain Res LBP-delay
  papers are paywalled beyond their abstracts; the exact millisecond delay value in LBP patients
  could **not** be independently verified in this search and should not be quoted as a number
  without full-text access — treat "significantly delayed" as qualitative-only pending full text).
- Hodges, Gandevia & Richardson 1997 (*J Appl Physiol*): respiratory loading manoeuvres shifted
  abdominal-to-deltoid activation latency by **31–107 ms** depending on condition — i.e. the
  feedforward timing budget is itself on the order of tens to ~100 ms, and is measurably perturbed
  by concurrent respiratory demand [18].
- **A contradictory/nuancing result:** Mehta et al. 2017 tested a specific sub-group of
  non-specific LBP patients *clinically diagnosed with movement coordination impairment* and found
  trunk postural muscle timing was **not** compromised (p > .05 for onset/offset latencies, burst
  duration, co-contraction duration) — i.e. the classic delayed-TrA finding does **not** generalise
  to all LBP presentations; motor control deficits in LBP are heterogeneous [60].
- Tangential, not TrA: Takasaki & Kawazoe 2020 found internal oblique onset-latency differed by
  **46.32 ± 70.83 ms** between high- and low-scoring Functional Movement Screen groups during a
  leg-raise task [61]; Zheng et al. 2021 measured TrA/IO "anticipatory delay" changes with
  whole-body vibration training but no ms value was recoverable from the abstract [62].
- Fine-wire-based feedforward work in a shoulder-movement paradigm (Yamane et al. 2022) confirms
  the general feedforward architecture extends further, to **psoas major, quadratus lumborum, TrA
  and lumbar multifidus together**, all activating ahead of deltoid onset with direction-dependent
  variability, using fine-wire electrodes explicitly because these muscles are "difficult to
  record" with surface electrodes [31].

**No golf-specific TrA feedforward-timing study exists.** The closest golf-adjacent data is
Skibski's ultrasound work (§5, §8), which measures TrA *thickness/activation ratio*, not onset
*timing*, and not during the true feedforward window before the downswing starts.

## 5. Golf-specific evidence — IAP, diaphragm, pelvic floor, breathing, and deep-core-adjacent LBP work

**Direct answer: no golf-specific IAP, diaphragm, or pelvic-floor study of any kind exists in this
search.** Multiple independently-phrased Europe PMC and PubMed queries (`golf AND intra-abdominal
pressure`, `golf AND diaphragm`, `golf AND pelvic floor`, `golf AND breathing`, `diaphragm
electromyography golf swing`) returned **zero** matches. This is a genuine, confirmed absence, not
a search-tooling failure — the same queries reliably surface adjacent literature (e.g. core-training
reviews that *mention* golf in passing) when relevant material exists.

**The one genuine exception — and it is a real one — is transversus abdominis, measured by
ultrasound, in golfers specifically, by a single research group (Skibski et al., University of
Central Florida READY Lab):**

- **Skibski, Devorski & Mangum 2022** (*J Vis Exp*, methods paper): describes a protocol for
  strapping an ultrasound transducer to the abdominal wall with an elastic belt and recording
  **during an actual golf swing**, then using frame-by-frame video analysis to correlate abdominal
  wall thickness change with swing phase. This is explicitly framed around the **external and
  internal obliques** ("crucial for rotational movements in golf") with instructed contraction
  timed to the start of the downswing, and demonstrates that ultrasound-biofeedback-plus-cueing
  increases muscle thickness more than verbal cueing alone [43]. **This is the only method in the
  entire search that captures deep/lateral abdominal wall muscle behaviour during a real,
  full-effort golf swing** — not a surrogate lab task.
- **Skibski, Burkholder & Goetschius 2020** (*Phys Ther Sport*, n=32 collegiate golfers, 16 with
  LBP history [4.6 ± 4.5 episodes/year] vs 16 without [0.1 ± 0.3 episodes/year], crossover design):
  measured TrA activity via ultrasound during a **static abdominal draw-in manoeuvre (ADIM)**, not
  during the dynamic swing itself. Findings: **no significant baseline difference** in TrA activity
  between the LBP and non-LBP golfer groups; TrA activity was **greater during real-time ultrasound
  biofeedback contractions than standard (unguided) contractions**; and — the most golf-specific
  finding — TrA activity was **lower in the golf-setup (address) posture than in supine**, i.e. the
  addressed/bent-over golf posture appears to *reduce* the muscle's baseline engageability relative
  to a standard clinical testing position [42].
- **Skibski, Stout, Ingersoll & Mangum 2024** (*Clin J Sport Med*, n=16 golfers with ≥2 LBP
  episodes in the past year): combined an ADIM (for TrA) with **actual golf swings** (for oblique
  activation), with and without real-time ultrasound biofeedback. TrA activation ratios were
  **significantly higher with biofeedback, bilaterally (p < 0.001)**; oblique activation ratios
  during the *golf swing itself* also improved with biofeedback (lead side p = 0.014, trail side
  p < 0.001); intra-rater reliability was excellent (ICC 0.92–0.97, p < 0.001) [41].

**What this trio does and does not establish:** it establishes that (a) TrA and oblique
*engageability* is trainable/measurable in golfers with a LBP history using real-time ultrasound,
and (b) the golf address posture itself measurably suppresses TrA engagement relative to supine —
a genuinely golf-specific biomechanical finding. It does **not** establish TrA (or any deep
stabiliser) activation timing or amplitude *during the swing itself* relative to swing phase — only
the oblique layer was tracked through the actual swing, and only as a thickness/qualitative
biofeedback target, not as a quantified %MVC or %EMGmax curve. **No diaphragm or pelvic floor
measurement appears anywhere in the Skibski series or elsewhere in golf.**

**Golf-and-LBP studies that discuss deep-core function without measuring it directly:**
- Cole & Grimshaw (cited already in `02-trunk-and-core-activation.md`) comment interpretively that
  LBP golfers appear to rely on erector spinae "rather than deeper spinal muscles like transversus
  abdominis and multifidus" — an inference from superficial-muscle EMG patterns, not a direct deep-
  muscle measurement.
- Hamada et al. 2025 (RCT, n=45 adolescent golfers): a "Golfers' Low back pain Exercise prevention
  Program" improved hip rotation ROM and trunk endurance (p < .001) and lead hip external-rotation
  strength (p = .017); no direct deep-core measurement, but trunk-endurance testing is a superficial
  proxy some clinicians treat as core-function evidence [27].
- Grathwohl & Sillevis 2024 (case report, 78-year-old golfer with LBP): TPI movement-screen-guided
  lumbar stabilisation exercises improved TPI composite score and reduced LBP; again, no direct
  deep-muscle measurement [28].
- Chen, Pandy, Huang & Tang 2024 (n=21 golfers): overhead-squat-test performance predicted lumbar
  extension kinematics and L4–S1 shear force during the downswing — a movement-quality proxy for
  trunk-control capacity, not a deep-core EMG/pressure measurement [29].
- Thomas & Wilk 2023 (clinical commentary): notes low back injuries are the most common golf
  injury category after overuse generally; recommends core-inclusive exercise progressions without
  citing deep-stabiliser measurement data [30].
- Becker & Kohlrieser 2014 (case report, professional golfer, sports hernia/groin pain): describes
  a "core muscle retraining program" using a neurodevelopmental-sequence progression; no
  instrumented deep-core measurement, purely clinical/functional [26].

**Bottom line for this section:** golf has real-time ultrasound TrA/oblique data (Skibski et al.,
three papers, one research group, all in golfers with an LBP history) — this is the single genuine
golf-specific deep-core data point in the literature. Diaphragm and pelvic floor remain **completely
unmeasured in golf, at any level, by any method.**

## 6. Breathing pattern during the golf swing — breath-holding, exhalation timing, Valsalva

**No published measurement of breathing pattern, breath-holding, exhalation timing, or Valsalva
manoeuvre during an actual golf swing was found anywhere in this search**, despite multiple query
angles (`golf swing breathing respiration`, `golf swing breath hold Valsalva`, `breath holding
trunk stability rotational power baseball pitching tennis serve`).

- The single golf study that measures anything respiratory is **van der Lei & Tenenbaum 2012**
  (*Appl Psychophysiol Biofeedback*), which recorded **heart rate and respiration rate** in golfers
  as objective physiological markers of psychological arousal within "affect-related performance
  zones." This is a **psychophysiology/arousal study, not a biomechanics/breathing-mechanics
  study** — it does not address breath-holding, Valsalva, or exhalation timing relative to swing
  phase in any way [63].
- No equivalent measurement was found in **any other striking or throwing sport either**
  (baseball pitching, tennis serve) — the search for breath-holding/Valsalva specifically during a
  rotational striking/throwing action returned only unrelated conference-proceedings supplements.
  This is a genuine gap across the whole of rotational-sport biomechanics, not a golf-specific
  omission.
- What *does* exist, and is the only legitimate quantitative anchor for a Valsalva-type strategy in
  any athletic task, is the general resistance-exercise IAP/Valsalva literature already covered in
  §1 (Harman 1988 [51], Blazek 2019 [50]) — these establish that **voluntary breath-holding via
  Valsalva manoeuvre is a known, measured strategy for raising IAP during near-maximal lifting**,
  and that IAP under Valsalva (up to ~200–277 mmHg) is categorically higher than IAP under
  unrestricted breathing during the same lift. Extrapolating this mechanism to golf (a much lower
  peak-force, much higher-velocity task) is plausible in principle — momentary breath-holding or
  timed exhalation around impact would be consistent with the same stiffening mechanism — **but
  this is extrapolation, not golf evidence**, and should not be reported as a measured golf finding.
- Popular golf-coaching literature (Titleist Performance Institute and similar) makes claims about
  exhaling through impact or bracing the core pre-downswing; **no peer-reviewed measurement of
  this claim, in golf or in any closely analogous striking/throwing sport, was located.**

## 7. How IAP and deep-core activity is measured — and why surface EMG cannot capture it

**Fine-wire / intramuscular EMG.** TrA, multifidus, quadratus lumborum, psoas major, and the
diaphragm all require indwelling fine-wire (intramuscular) electrodes for valid recording:
- TrA lies **deep to** internal oblique and external oblique; a surface electrode over the
  lateral abdominal wall inevitably records a **blended, cross-talk-contaminated signal**
  dominated by the more superficial oblique layers, not an isolated TrA signal [02-trunk doc,
  corroborated methodologically by 31, 32, 33].
- Weber et al. 2017 and De Martino et al. 2020 (both Hodges-group studies) used combined
  intramuscular fine-wire *and* surface EMG specifically because deep lumbo-pelvic stabilisers
  (multifidus, TrA, quadratus lumborum, iliocostalis, longissimus) "require specialized electrode
  placement for accurate signal capture" that surface electrodes cannot provide [32, 33].
- Yamane et al. 2022 used fine-wire electrodes for psoas major, quadratus lumborum, TrA and
  lumbar multifidus explicitly because these structures are "difficult to record" with surface
  methods, while using ordinary surface electrodes for the superficial muscles in the same study —
  a direct within-study demonstration of why the two techniques are not interchangeable [31].
- The diaphragm is typically accessed via **oesophageal or intramuscular (needle) electrodes**, or
  inferred indirectly from crural diaphragm EMG via a swallowed multi-pair oesophageal electrode
  catheter, in the Hodges/Gandevia body of work [1–6]; none of this is compatible with unrestricted
  full-speed athletic movement, which is precisely why no golf diaphragm EMG study exists.
- The **pelvic floor** is accessed via intravaginal or intra-anal surface/needle electrodes or
  probes (Sapsford et al. used vaginal EMG electrodes) [44] — again incompatible with a free, full-
  speed golf swing without significant apparatus and participant burden.

**IAP itself is measured via pressure catheter, not EMG.**
- The classical biomechanics tradition (Hodges, Cholewicki, McGill, Cresswell, Stokes) uses an
  **intragastric pressure catheter/transducer**, typically a naso-gastric or swallowed balloon
  catheter, sometimes paired with an intra-oesophageal catheter to separate intra-abdominal from
  intra-thoracic pressure components [1, 51 and others throughout §1].
- The critical-care/surgical literature instead treats **intravesical (bladder) catheter pressure**
  as the clinical gold standard, validated against intragastric pressure with only moderate
  agreement (femoral-venous-vs-intragastric bias −0.8 ± 4.4 mmHg, limits of agreement −9.6/+8.0
  mmHg, percentage error 55%; femoral-venous-vs-intravesical bias +0.5 ± 4.2 mmHg, LOA −7.9/+8.9,
  PE 51% — i.e. **none of the indirect surrogates agree closely enough with each other to be
  treated as interchangeable**) [46, 47].
- Emerging non-invasive alternatives (ingestible wireless capsule pressure sensors) show good
  correlation with intravesical measurement (ICC 0.916, mean bias 0.6 mmHg, SD 1.68 mmHg) but
  remain a clinical-monitoring tool (post-surgical patients at rest/low activity), not validated
  for a full-speed dynamic athletic movement [48, 49].

**Why this means golf data may never exist, or only very narrowly:**
- Fine-wire EMG of TrA/multifidus/diaphragm and intragastric/intravesical pressure catheterisation
  are both **invasive, apparatus-heavy, and physically constraining** (a nasogastric tube, an
  indwelling needle electrode, or a bladder catheter is not compatible with an unrestricted,
  full-effort competitive-speed golf swing without meaningful risk of injury, discomfort-driven
  swing alteration, or dislodgement).
- The Skibski et al. series (§5, §8) demonstrates the one currently viable compromise: **surface
  ultrasound imaging**, which is non-invasive and swing-compatible (secured with an elastic belt),
  but which measures **muscle thickness change**, an indirect and imperfect proxy for activation
  (§8), and only reaches the oblique layer with confidence during a true dynamic swing — TrA
  itself was only measured statically (ADIM), not through the live swing, in all three Skibski
  papers.
- **Realistic assessment:** fine-wire EMG of TrA/multifidus and any direct IAP catheter measurement
  during a live, full-speed competitive golf swing is very unlikely ever to be attempted — the
  instrumentation burden is high relative to the (currently small) golf-biomechanics research
  base, and no group doing fine-wire deep-spinal-muscle work (Hodges, Cholewicki, McGill,
  Cresswell/Thorstensson lineages) has ever published a golf study. Diaphragm and pelvic floor
  measurement in golf is **realistically even less likely** than TrA/multifidus, given the
  additional apparatus burden (oesophageal/intramuscular diaphragm electrodes; intravaginal/
  intra-anal pelvic floor electrodes) is more invasive still. Ultrasound-based *thickness-proxy*
  studies (the Skibski model) are the one plausible near-term route to *any* further golf-specific
  deep-core data, and even that has only been applied to obliques/TrA so far, never diaphragm or
  pelvic floor.

## 8. Ultrasound-based studies of transversus abdominis / diaphragm in athletes, including golf

**Golf (already detailed in §5):** Skibski et al. — three ultrasound papers on TrA/oblique
thickness-based activation in golfers, one measuring during a live swing (obliques only) [41, 42,
43].

**Non-golf athlete/clinical ultrasound TrA studies, with numeric values where recoverable:**
- Reliability/validity of the **"preferential activation ratio" (PAR)** — a standard measure of
  TrA thickness-change relative to overlying obliques — is well established methodologically
  (Perotti et al. 2025: positive PAR values in both older-adult study arms during ADIM/bracing;
  Valentín-Mazarracin et al. 2021: manual palpation vs. TrA-ratio correlation only **r = 0.36–0.60**
  (low-to-moderate), best-predictive-model ROC 0.89, sensitivity 0.95–1.00, specificity 0.62 at a
  **TrA-PAR threshold ≥ 0.08**) [36].
- Meta-analytic reliability of RUSI (rehabilitative ultrasound imaging) in the lumbopelvic region:
  **low heterogeneity for TrA thickness measurement specifically (I² = 22%)**, versus much higher
  heterogeneity for multifidus (I² = 95%) and for LBP-outcome measures generally (I² = 92%) — i.e.
  TrA thickness is one of the more *reliably measurable* ultrasound targets in this literature,
  even though what that thickness change *means* functionally is contested [37].
- **Explicit validity-limit evidence that thickness change ≠ activation level:** Daigle et al. 2022
  found **no between-group difference in TrA activation ratio** after biofeedback training in
  older adults in supine or standing, and — counterintuitively — **TrA thickness change was
  significantly *less*, not more, post-intervention** in the RUSI-feedback group in standing;
  the authors concluded neither RUSI nor pressure biofeedback achieved the intended TrA activation
  effect. This is a direct empirical demonstration that a *training intervention aimed at
  increasing thickness change can fail to increase thickness change, or can even reduce it, while
  the clinician's target (activation) is not verified by the ultrasound proxy at all* [35].
- Cervera-Cano et al. 2024's LBP case-control ultrasound protocol measured TrA, lumbar multifidus,
  and diaphragm/pelvic-floor excursion *together*, finding significant between-group differences in
  a sitting-contralateral-arm-raise manoeuvre specifically for the "transverse abdominis ratio"
  (p = 0.033, r = 0.275) [24].

**Diaphragm ultrasound in athletes (thickness at total lung capacity, or thickening fraction, as
the standard measure):**
- Professional dancers (Aksu et al. 2026): inspiratory muscle training (8 weeks, 60% of maximal
  inspiratory pressure) produced significant increases in diaphragm thickness at total lung
  capacity [source found via search 1 — "Aksu H"].
- Meta-regression across populations (Eyipınar et al. 2026): inspiratory muscle training increases
  diaphragm thickness with a pooled effect size **Hedges' g = 0.52 (95% CI 0.19–0.85)** [see
  search-log entry for Eyipınar; PMID 41901690, PMCID PMC13027613].
- Professional basketball players (Ladriñán-Maestro et al. 2026): a fatigue protocol produced an
  **11% decrease** in inspiratory diaphragm thickness, while a warm-up protocol produced a **9%
  increase** — i.e. diaphragm thickness is acutely state-dependent (fatigue vs. warm-up), not a
  fixed trait value, which matters for interpreting any single-session ultrasound snapshot [39].
- Older women, resistance training (Akkuş Uçar et al. 2025): **16.66% increase** in diaphragm
  thickness after training versus **1.13% decrease** in controls over 4 weeks [40].
- Systematic review of diaphragmatic ultrasonography across sports disciplines (Payán-Salcedo et
  al. 2024) found a **positive correlation between diaphragm excursion/thickness during
  inspiration and anaerobic power** across the disciplines reviewed — **golf was not among the
  disciplines represented** [38].
- Bodybuilders (Güler et al. 2025, natural bodybuilders): inspiratory muscle training studied for
  diaphragm-thickness and strength adaptations, with the authors noting diaphragm contribution "to
  power generation by optimising intra-abdominal pressure" as a stated rationale — but, as with
  other entries here, **no actual IAP values were measured or reported**, only asserted as
  rationale [found via search — Güler I et al., *Front Physiol* 2025, PMID 40832136].

**Known validity limits of thickness change as an activation proxy (synthesised across §8):**
1. Thickness-change and manual-palpation/clinical assessment correlate only weakly-to-moderately
   (r = 0.36–0.60) [36].
2. A biofeedback intervention can fail to move thickness change in the intended direction even when
   an activation-ratio outcome is unaffected, showing the two measures can dissociate [35].
3. Diaphragm thickness is acutely state-dependent (fatigue reduces it, warm-up increases it, by
   double-digit percentages within a single session), so a single ultrasound snapshot cannot be
   assumed to represent a stable trait-level activation capacity [39].
4. Ultrasound thickness change is a **static or quasi-static-position proxy** in almost every study
   located here (supine, standing, ADIM) — only the Skibski oblique work has been extended to a
   genuinely dynamic, full-speed athletic movement, and even there it is thickness-on-video read
   qualitatively frame-by-frame, not a validated %MVC-equivalent output [43].

## 9. Co-contraction and spinal stiffness in rotation specifically

This is the most direct mechanistic link to the golf swing's actual demand (axial torque against
the spine), and it is also the **thinnest evidentially** of the nine sections — most of the
Cholewicki/McGill/Hodges stiffness literature quantifies **flexion and lateral bending**
specifically; rotation/torsional stiffness numbers are comparatively rare.

- **Cresswell, Grundström & Thorstensson 1992** (intramuscular electrodes + intragastric pressure):
  during trunk-twisting movements, TrA showed **reciprocal left/right activity patterns** — i.e.
  the two sides of TrA do not co-contract symmetrically during rotation the way they do during
  flexion, but instead alternate/oppose — "indicating an ability for torque development." This is
  the clearest direct evidence in this search that TrA participates specifically in **generating
  and resisting rotational torque**, not just symmetrical bracing [53].
- **Cresswell, Oddsson & Thorstensson 1994** (sudden perturbations while standing): TrA "was always
  the first muscle active" in both unexpected external perturbations and self-induced (loaded-vest)
  perturbations, with IAP elevation functioning as "a mechanism designed to improve the stability
  of the trunk through a stiffening of the whole segment" — i.e. the stiffening mechanism is
  general-purpose (works against unpredicted multi-directional loading, which includes rotational
  components), not flexion-specific [55].
- **Stokes et al. 2010** modelled IAP-driven spinal unloading across **flexion, extension, lateral
  bending, and rotation** together at IAP levels of 5 and 10 kPa, but the abstract-level detail
  recovered here does not break out a rotation-specific numeric stiffness or unloading value
  separate from the other directions [15] — flagged as a genuine gap: the paper likely contains a
  rotation-specific number in the full text that could not be extracted from the abstract alone.
- **No numeric torsional stiffness value (Nm/rad or equivalent, specifically for axial rotation)**
  attributable to abdominal-wall or IAP contribution was recoverable from any abstract in this
  search, despite multiple targeted queries (`spine torsional stiffness abdominal muscle
  co-activation rotation`, `trunk muscle co-contraction spinal stiffness axial rotation torque`,
  `intra-abdominal pressure trunk rotation axial twisting task`). The Brown & McGill and Cholewicki
  programmes' well-quantified stiffness numbers (§1) are specifically flexion/lateral-bend
  figures; a like-for-like rotational number (e.g. "co-contraction increases torsional stiffness by
  X Nm/rad" or "by X%") was not found and should be treated as **absent from the accessible
  literature**, not merely unextracted.
- Indirect supporting logic: Granata & Orishimo 2001's stability model shows antagonistic
  co-contraction increases with the *potential energy* of the loaded system even at constant
  external moment [source log — Granata/Orishimo entry] — a general principle (more instability
  risk → more co-contraction, regardless of load direction) that plausibly extends to rotation but
  was not tested in rotation specifically in the paper as abstracted.
- Golf-adjacent, non-deep-core evidence: Chen, Pandy, Huang & Tang 2024 found golfers with better
  overhead-squat-test performance had smaller lumbar-extension angular velocity and smaller
  L4–S1 joint shear force during the downswing [29] — consistent with better trunk-control
  athletes producing lower net spinal loading during the golf swing's rotational demand, but this
  is a whole-body movement-quality correlate, not a deep-core stiffness mechanism measurement.

**Honest gap statement:** the golf swing's dominant spinal demand is axial torque, and this is
precisely the loading direction with the **least-quantified** deep-core stiffness data of any
direction in the entire non-golf literature reviewed here. Flexion and lateral-bend stiffness
contributions from IAP/TrA are numerically well characterised (§1); rotational stiffness
contributions are asserted mechanistically (Cresswell 1992's reciprocal-activity finding) but not
numerically quantified in any source recovered in this search.
