## 1. Origin, insertion, primary action — by muscle

### Hip / thigh

**Gluteus maximus** [S1]
- O: posterior gluteal line of outer ilium; lower sacrum/side of coccyx; sacrotuberous ligament; lumbar fascia.
- I: iliotibial band of fascia lata (upper/superficial fibres); gluteal tuberosity of linea aspera (deep fibres).
- A: hip — extension (primary), external rotation, abduction (upper fibres); knee — extension assist via IT tract; pelvis stabilisation in stance, decelerates trunk flexion.

**Gluteus medius** [S2]
- O: gluteal surface of ilium (under gluteus maximus), gluteal aponeurosis.
- I: greater trochanter of femur.
- A: anterior fibres flex + internally rotate hip; posterior fibres extend + externally rotate hip; whole muscle abducts hip and stabilises pelvis in coronal plane (prevents contralateral pelvic drop). Function is position-dependent.

**Adductor magnus** [S3]
- O: ischiopubic ramus (pubis/ischium) — adductor part; ischial tuberosity — hamstring (ischiocondylar) part.
- I: linea aspera of femur (adductor part); adductor tubercle of medial femoral condyle (hamstring part).
- A: both parts adduct hip. Adductor (pubofemoral) part: hip flexion assist + lateral rotation. Ischiocondylar (hamstring) part: hip extension + medial rotation. Different nerve supply per part (obturator vs sciatic) reflects the split function.

**Biceps femoris** [S4]
- Long head — O: ischial tuberosity; I: fibular head; A: hip extension + (with short head) knee flexion and lateral tibial rotation when knee flexed. Crosses hip and knee (biarticular).
- Short head — O: linea aspera/lateral supracondylar line of femur; I: fibular head; A: knee flexion + lateral tibial rotation; monoarticular (knee only).

**Semitendinosus** [S4]
- O: ischial tuberosity. I: medial surface of proximal tibia (pes anserinus).
- A: hip extension + knee flexion + medial tibial rotation when knee flexed. Biarticular.

**Semimembranosus** [S4]
- O: ischial tuberosity. I: medial tibial condyle (posteromedial).
- A: hip extension, knee flexion, medial tibial rotation when knee flexed. Biarticular.

**Rectus femoris** [S5]
- O: anterior inferior iliac spine + superior acetabular rim. I: tibial tuberosity via patella/patellar ligament.
- A: knee extension + hip flexion. Biarticular (only quadriceps component crossing both hip and knee).

**Vasti (vastus lateralis, medialis, intermedius)** [S5]
- O: lateral, medial and anterior/proximal shaft of femur respectively (all monoarticular, arise from femur not pelvis). I: tibial tuberosity via patella (vastus medialis also has patellar-stabilising oblique fibres, VMO).
- A: knee extension only; no hip action (unlike rectus femoris).

### Lower leg

**Gastrocnemius** [S6]
- O: medial head — medial femoral condyle; lateral head — lateral femoral condyle.
- I: calcaneus via Achilles (calcaneal) tendon (shared with soleus).
- A: ankle plantarflexion + knee flexion. Biarticular (knee + ankle, technically crosses subtalar joint too). Fast-twitch-dominant, suited to ballistic movement.

**Soleus** [S7]
- O: posterior fibula + soleal line of medial tibia.
- I: calcaneus via Achilles tendon.
- A: ankle plantarflexion only. Monoarticular (ankle only) — unlike gastrocnemius, unaffected by knee position.

**Tibialis anterior** [S8]
- O: upper 2/3 of lateral tibial shaft + interosseous membrane.
- I: medial cuneiform + base of 1st metatarsal.
- A: ankle dorsiflexion (largest dorsiflexor) + foot inversion. Eccentrically controls plantarflexion (foot-strike lowering) and stabilises ankle in stance; concentrically clears the foot in swing.

**Peroneus longus (fibularis longus)** [S9]
- O: head + upper 2/3 of lateral fibula.
- I: lateral side of base of 1st metatarsal + medial cuneiform (crosses sole).
- A: ankle eversion (opposes tibialis anterior) + plantarflexion assist; supports transverse arch; stabilises leg over foot in single-leg stance.

### Trunk — posterior/deep

**Erector spinae (iliocostalis, longissimus, spinalis; thoracic + lumbar)** [S10]
- O: sacrum, iliac crest, lower thoracic/lumbar vertebrae (common origin as far down as the pelvis).
- I: iliocostalis → ribs; longissimus → transverse processes + ribs; spinalis → spinous processes; upper fibres reach cervical/upper thoracic vertebrae and ribs.
- A: bilateral contraction — spinal extension (straightens back). Unilateral contraction — lateral flexion AND rotation to the SAME (ipsilateral) side.

**Multifidus (lumbar)** [S11]
- O: sacrum (posterior), lumbar mamillary processes, thoracic transverse processes, cervical articular processes (segmental, deep to erector spinae).
- I: spinous process of the vertebra 2-4 levels above each origin (oblique upward/medial fascicles).
- A: bilateral — spinal extension. Unilateral — ipsilateral side-bending WITH CONTRALATERAL (opposite-side) rotation. High spindle density → proprioceptive/segmental stabiliser role. Note this rotation direction is opposite in sign convention from erector spinae's ipsilateral rotation — the two deep-back groups do not rotate the same way.

**Quadratus lumborum** [S12]
- O: iliac crest (iliolumbar ligament region)/wings of ilium.
- I: transverse processes of L1–L4 + inferior border of 12th rib.
- A: unilateral — lateral flexion of lumbar spine to the same side + hip-hike (pelvis elevation) on that side. Bilateral — lumbar extension + trunk/pelvis stabilisation. Also fixes 12th rib for forced expiration and assists inspiration.

### Shoulder girdle / arm

**Latissimus dorsi** [S23]
- O: spinous processes T7–S5, thoracolumbar fascia, iliac crest, inferior 4 ribs, inferior angle of scapula.
- I: floor of intertubercular (bicipital) groove of humerus.
- A: shoulder extension, adduction, internal rotation. With arms fixed overhead, assists trunk rotation and lateral flexion by pulling the trunk toward the fixed arm.

**Pectoralis major** [S24]
- O: clavicular head — anterior medial half of clavicle; sternocostal head — anterior sternum + superior 6 costal cartilages + external oblique aponeurosis.
- I: lateral lip of the intertubercular (bicipital) groove of humerus (~5 cm wide flat tendon).
- A: clavicular head — shoulder flexion + horizontal adduction assist. Sternocostal head — shoulder adduction + internal rotation + extension (from a flexed position). Whole muscle: adducts and medially rotates humerus, draws scapula anteroinferiorly. Independent dual innervation (medial/lateral pectoral nerves) allows the two heads to act semi-independently.

**Deltoid — three heads** [S25]
- Anterior (clavicular): O lateral third of clavicle (anterior border/upper surface); I deltoid tuberosity of humerus; A shoulder flexion + internal rotation (synergist with pec major/subscapularis).
- Middle (acromial): O acromion (superior surface); I deltoid tuberosity; A shoulder abduction (prime abductor past ~15°); positioned to resist inferior humeral head translation.
- Posterior (spinal): O spine of scapula (lower lip of posterior border); I deltoid tuberosity; A shoulder extension + external rotation; antagonist to the strong internal rotators (pec major/lat dorsi/subscapularis).

**Rotator cuff — four muscles** [S26]
- Supraspinatus — O supraspinous fossa; I superior facet of greater tubercle; A shoulder abduction (initiates/assists first ~15°, works throughout with deltoid), dynamic humeral head stabiliser.
- Infraspinatus — O infraspinous fossa; I middle facet of greater tubercle; A shoulder external rotation.
- Subscapularis — O subscapular fossa (anterior scapula); I lesser tubercle of humerus; A shoulder internal rotation; largest/strongest cuff muscle, major anterior stabiliser.
- Teres minor — O middle 1/3 of lateral scapular border; I inferior facet of greater tubercle; A shoulder external rotation, synergist with infraspinatus.

**Trapezius — three parts** [S27]
- Upper (descending): O medial 1/3 superior nuchal line, external occipital protuberance, C7; I posterior/lateral clavicle; A scapular elevation + upward rotation assist, supports arm weight.
- Middle (transverse): O spinous processes C7–T3; I acromion + scapular spine; A scapular retraction.
- Lower (ascending): O spinous processes T4–T12; I apex of scapular spine via aponeurosis; A scapular depression + upward rotation assist. Upper + lower fibres act together as a force couple for scapular upward rotation (overhead movement).

**Rhomboids (major + minor)** [S28]
- O: nuchal ligament + spinous processes C7–T5. I: medial border of scapula.
- A: scapular retraction, downward rotation of glenoid cavity, stabilise scapula against thorax.

**Levator scapulae** [S29]
- O: posterior tubercles of transverse processes C1–C4. I: medial border of scapula, superior angle to scapular-spine junction.
- A: scapular elevation (spine fixed) + downward rotation, working with rhomboids/pec minor. With scapula fixed: ipsilateral cervical rotation + lateral flexion.

**Serratus anterior** [S30]
- O: fleshy slips from outer surface of ribs 1–9 (~10 slips). I: costal (anterior) aspect of medial border of scapula, superior to inferior angle.
- A: scapular protraction ("boxer's muscle") + upward rotation (inferior fibres, paired with trapezius force couple to allow full arm elevation) + holds scapula against thoracic wall (prevents winging).

### Arm and forearm

**Biceps brachii** [S31]
- O: short head — coracoid process of scapula; long head — supraglenoid tubercle (via intra-articular tendon over the humeral head).
- I: radial tuberosity + bicipital aponeurosis into forearm fascia.
- A: elbow flexion (strongest when forearm supinated) + powerful forearm supination + weak shoulder flexion (long head). Long head is biarticular (shoulder + elbow); primary functional role is at the elbow.

**Triceps brachii** [S32]
- O: long head — infraglenoid tubercle of scapula; lateral head — proximal humerus above radial groove; medial head — distal humerus below radial groove.
- I: olecranon process of ulna (common tendon, all 3 heads).
- A: elbow extension (all heads). Long head additionally biarticular — assists shoulder extension + adduction from its scapular origin. Lateral/medial heads are monoarticular (elbow only).

**Brachioradialis** [S33]
- O: lateral supracondylar ridge of humerus. I: distal radius (radial styloid process).
- A: elbow flexion — strongest in forearm neutral (mid-pronation/supination) position; secondary rotational action pulls the forearm toward neutral from either extreme (assists supination when pronated, assists pronation when supinated).

**Pronator teres** [S34]
- O: humeral head — medial supracondylar ridge/medial epicondyle (common flexor origin); ulnar head — coronoid process of ulna.
- I: middle of lateral surface of radial shaft.
- A: forearm pronation (primary) + weak elbow flexion assist under resistance. Antagonist: supinator.

**Supinator** [S35]
- O: lateral epicondyle of humerus, supinator crest of ulna, radial collateral + annular ligaments.
- I: proximal lateral radial shaft (wraps around radius).
- A: forearm supination only — no elbow action. Unlike biceps brachii, remains effective across the full range of elbow flexion/extension; is the prime mover for unresisted/unloaded supination, with biceps recruited additionally under load.

**Wrist flexor group** (flexor carpi radialis, flexor carpi ulnaris, palmaris longus) [S36]
- O: common flexor origin — medial epicondyle of humerus.
- I: flexor carpi radialis → bases of 2nd/3rd metacarpals; flexor carpi ulnaris → pisiform/hamate/5th metacarpal (not directly sourced here, standard anatomy); palmaris longus → palmar aponeurosis.
- A: wrist flexion (all three); flexor carpi radialis adds radial deviation, flexor carpi ulnaris adds ulnar deviation — the group's net frontal-plane action depends on which member is dominant at a given moment.

**Wrist extensor group** (extensor carpi radialis longus, extensor carpi radialis brevis, extensor carpi ulnaris, extensor digitorum) [S37]
- O: common extensor origin — lateral epicondyle of humerus (ECRL arises slightly more proximally, from the lateral supracondylar ridge).
- I: extensor carpi radialis longus → base of 2nd metacarpal; others insert on 3rd/5th metacarpals and finger extensor mechanisms per standard anatomy.
- A: wrist extension (all); ECRL/ECRB add radial deviation, ECU adds ulnar deviation. Innervated by radial nerve (ECRL directly; others via posterior interosseous branch).

### Trunk — anterior/lateral abdominal wall

**Rectus abdominis** [S13]
- O: pubic crest/pubic symphysis. I: costal cartilages of ribs 5–7 + xiphoid process.
- A: trunk (lumbar spine) flexion only — pulls rib cage toward pelvis or pelvis toward rib cage. No rotation function. Assists forced expiration and raises intra-abdominal pressure.

**External oblique** [S14]
- O: outer surfaces of ribs 5–12. I: xiphoid process, linea alba, pubic crest/tubercle, outer lip of iliac crest, ASIS, inguinal ligament.
- A: bilateral — trunk flexion + compression. Unilateral — **ipsilateral side-bending + CONTRALATERAL (opposite-side) rotation.** Verbatim: "the right external oblique would side-bend to the right and rotate to the left, and vice versa."

**Internal oblique** [S15]
- O: thoracolumbar fascia, anterior 2/3 of iliac crest, lateral half of inguinal ligament.
- I: inferior borders of ribs 10–12, linea alba.
- A: bilateral — abdominal compression. Unilateral — **ipsilateral (same-side) rotation + ipsilateral side-bending** (termed "same-side rotators"). Verbatim: "the right internal oblique and the left external oblique contract as the torso flexes and rotates to bring the left shoulder towards the right hip" — i.e. right IO + left EO both drive rightward trunk rotation; the functional pair for a given rotation direction is one side's internal oblique + the OPPOSITE side's external oblique.

**Transversus abdominis** [S16]
- O: lateral third of inguinal ligament, anterior 3/4 of inner iliac crest, inner surfaces of costal cartilages of ribs 7–12, thoracolumbar fascia.
- I: linea alba (upper fibres); pubic crest/pectineal line via conjoint tendon (lower fibres).
- A: primarily compresses abdomen/viscera for thoracic and pelvic stability (corset action), not a primary flexor or rotator; intra-abdominal pressure and forced expiration. Some unilateral ipsilateral rotation contribution reported but secondary to its stabilising role.

## 2. Trunk axial rotation — the oblique directional rule

**The rule, stated precisely and cited:**
- **External oblique unilateral contraction → CONTRALATERAL trunk rotation** (rotates trunk to the opposite side) + ipsilateral side-bending. Right EO → rotates trunk left. [S14]
- **Internal oblique unilateral contraction → IPSILATERAL trunk rotation** (rotates trunk to the same side) + ipsilateral side-bending; internal obliques are explicitly termed "same-side rotators." Right IO → rotates trunk right. [S15]
- **Functional pairing:** a given rotation direction is produced by one side's external oblique acting together with the OPPOSITE side's internal oblique. Worked example, directly citable: "the right internal oblique and the left external oblique contract as the torso flexes and rotates to bring the left shoulder towards the right hip" [S15] — i.e. right IO + left EO co-contract to rotate the trunk to the right. By the same logic, **right EO + left IO co-contract to rotate the trunk to the LEFT** — exactly the pairing named in the task brief, and confirmed independently from both the EO article [S14] and the IO article [S15] (each names the other muscle's opposite-side partner for coordinated rotation).

**Which side drives a right-handed golfer's downswing rotation toward the target:** In the golf swing, backswing rotates the torso and pelvis AWAY from the target (for a right-handed golfer, this is a rightward/clockwise-from-above rotation); the downswing reverses this — the pelvis initiates rotation toward the target first, with the torso following, both rotating LEFT (toward the target) through impact, while X-factor stretch is created because the torso still lags/continues in the away-from-target direction briefly as the pelvis leads — sourced from a systematic review of golf swing kinematics [S17]. Applying the oblique rule above: trunk rotation toward the target (leftward, for a right-handed golfer) is driven by the **trail-side (right) external oblique working with the lead-side (left) internal oblique**. This is independently corroborated by this project's own prior EMG research: trail external oblique peaks in late downswing/acceleration (`docs/research/02-trunk-and-core-activation.md`, citing Bulbulian/Watkins-type EMG data), i.e. the muscle predicted to drive target-ward rotation is the one observed to be most active exactly when that rotation happens.
- Rectus abdominis produces flexion only, no rotation component [S13].
- Transversus abdominis is primarily a compressor/stabiliser, not a prime rotator; any rotation contribution is secondary [S16].
- Erector spinae, contracting unilaterally, rotates the trunk to the SAME (ipsilateral) side [S10] — opposite in sign to external oblique, same sign as internal oblique.
- Multifidus, contracting unilaterally, produces ipsilateral side-bending but CONTRALATERAL rotation [S11] — i.e. multifidus and erector spinae, despite both being posterior "extensor" muscles, rotate in opposite directions from each other; multifidus's rotation sign matches external oblique's, not erector spinae's.
- General (non-directional) confirmation that EO/IO/TA all contribute to trunk rotation and lateral bending in golfers specifically, from a golf low-back-pain biomechanics review [S18]; the same review found golfers with low back pain show reduced erector spinae activity during the swing versus asymptomatic golfers, and that gluteus medius acts eccentrically during the backswing while gluteus maximus stabilises the pelvis [S18].

## 3. Moment arms — published values

**Access note:** the classic primary sources that tabulate trunk-rotator moment arms in cm/mm
(Bogduk, Macintosh & Pearcy 1992; Stokes & Gardner-Morse 1999, *J Biomech* PMID 10093031; the
Christophy et al. 2012 lumbar-spine OpenSim model, PMID 21318374) are paywalled with no PMCID —
Europe PMC and PubMed surface only bibliographic metadata, not the numeric tables, across
repeated targeted searches. This mirrors F-015/F-033's documented pattern elsewhere in this
project (paywalled primary EMG tables). Two open-access musculoskeletal-model papers that cite
Bogduk 1992 as their anatomical source were reached in full text (Toumanidou & Noailly 2015,
PMC4525063; Bruno et al. 2015, PMC5101035) but neither tabulates rotation-specific moment arms
in its own text — Bruno et al. reports only RMSE-of-fit values (e.g. 0.36 cm AP, 0.34 cm ML for
erector spinae) against a reference model, not the moment arms themselves [S19, S20].

**Numeric values that were recovered directly, with joint-angle dependence:**
- **Knee, biceps femoris (long head):** flexion moment arm peaks at **3.0 cm at 55° knee
  flexion** — Arnold, Ward, Lieber & Delp 2010, *Ann Biomed Eng* 38(2):269-279, PMID 19957039,
  PMCID PMC2903973 (the anatomical/geometric basis of the widely used OpenSim gait2392 lower-limb
  model) [S21].
- **Knee, quadriceps (extensor moment arm of the modelled extensor mechanism):** peaks at
  **4.7 cm at 25° knee flexion** — same source [S21]. Both values demonstrate substantial,
  non-trivial moment-arm change across the knee's range of motion — a single "leverage" number
  per muscle is not physiologically valid without stating the joint angle.
- **Hip, internal/external rotation (8 muscle groups, 18 compartments: gluteus maximus ×6,
  gluteus medius ×4, gluteus minimus ×3, iliopsoas, piriformis, quadratus femoris, obturator
  internus, obturator externus):** rotation moment arms were measured by direct cadaveric
  tendon-excursion methods at hip flexion angles of 0°, 20°, 45°, 60° and 90° — Delp, Hess,
  Hungerford & Jones 1999, *J Biomech* 32(5):493-501, PMID 10327003 [S22]. Key qualitative
  finding (numeric table itself inaccessible — no PMCID): **moment arms change enough with hip
  flexion that muscles can switch rotation direction outright** — "the internal rotation moment
  arms of some muscles increase with flexion; the external rotation moment arms of other muscles
  decrease, and some muscles switch from external rotation to internal rotation as the hip is
  flexed." ~15 of 18 compartments show increased internal-rotation moment arm with flexion;
  gluteus maximus retains "a large capacity for external rotation" across the range [S22]. This
  is the single clearest evidence in this report that a fixed "muscle X does action Y" mapping
  is wrong for hip rotators once the joint is away from neutral — highly relevant to golf, where
  the trail hip is flexed and internally rotated for much of the swing.

**Which trunk muscles have the greatest rotational leverage (qualitative ranking, pending
numeric confirmation):** anatomically, external oblique and internal oblique have the largest
rotation-relevant moment arms among trunk muscles because their fibres run obliquely at a
substantial lateral distance from the spine's long axis (large lever arm relative to the
rotation axis), consistent with their being the primary named rotators in every source consulted
[S14, S15]. Multifidus and erector spinae also contribute rotation torque but are positioned much
closer to the vertebral rotation axis (short lever arm) and are conventionally understood as
extension-dominant with rotation as a secondary action [S10, S11] — this is stated directionally
by every anatomical source above but not yet found with a matching cm-level moment-arm number
from an accessible primary source; flagged as a gap (see final report gaps).

## 4. Biarticular muscles and activation-to-motion ambiguity

**Core problem, cited:** a biarticular muscle's net effect on motion is not determined by its
activation level alone — it depends on the moment-arm ratio at the two joints it spans, the
instantaneous joint configuration, and co-contraction with monoarticular synergists at either
joint — Schumacher, Sharbafi, Seyfarth & Rode 2020, "Biarticular muscles in light of template
models, experiments and robotics: a review," *J R Soc Interface* 17(163). PMID 32093540, PMCID
PMC7061696 [S38]. Directly: "if a biarticular muscle is co-contracted with an adjacent
monoarticular muscle, the monoarticular muscle can act on a joint that it does not span" — i.e.
two muscles can jointly cause a motion that neither would cause acting alone, which is
unrecoverable from single-muscle activation values in isolation. The paper states the
biarticular torque split between the two joints a muscle spans is set by "the ratio of their
muscle moment arms," so the same EMG amplitude produces different joint-level effects depending
on posture [S38].

**Lombard's paradox — the sharpest illustration:** the review cites this as the canonical case
where "a biarticular muscle [can] extend a joint that it anatomically flexes. The muscle extends
the joint through the action of a co-contracted biarticular antagonist" [S38]. Classically
demonstrated in rising from sit-to-stand: hamstrings (biarticular hip extensor/knee flexor) and
rectus femoris (biarticular hip flexor/knee extensor) co-contract, and the net result is
simultaneous hip AND knee extension — hamstrings appear to be "flexing" the knee by their
isolated anatomical action, but functionally contribute to knee extension in this coupled
task [S38]. This is a direct, citable proof that activation level cannot be mapped to a fixed
"this muscle does X" rule without knowing what else is contracting and in what posture.

**Biarticular muscles named as examples of the problem, cross-referenced against this report's
own muscle list** [S38]: rectus femoris (hip/knee — in this report's §1); hamstrings — biceps
femoris long head, semitendinosus, semimembranosus (hip/knee — §1); gastrocnemius (knee/ankle —
§1). Also biarticular in this report's own §1 findings, though not named in [S38] itself: biceps
brachii long head (shoulder/elbow, [S31]) and triceps brachii long head (shoulder/elbow, [S32]).
**Practical consequence for this app:** none of these six muscles can have their joint action
inferred from activation alone without also knowing the instantaneous angle at BOTH joints they
cross and the activation state of their monoarticular synergists — for a swing where every joint
is moving simultaneously and continuously, this is the single largest structural obstacle to a
naive "activation → colour → motion" rendering rule for these specific zones.

**Energy transfer compounds the ambiguity:** biarticular muscles can transfer mechanical energy
between the joints they span rather than simply producing torque locally — during jumping
push-off, energy flows from hip to ankle through biarticular coupling, and quick knee extension
can drive the biarticular gastrocnemius to convert rotational energy at the knee into
translational push-off power at the ankle, contributing up to 25% of peak power output at that
distal joint [S38]. A biarticular muscle can therefore be highly active while contributing motion
at a joint some distance from where naive anatomy would place its "action."

## 5. Eccentric vs concentric action — deceleration in striking/throwing sports

**Principle:** a muscle active while LENGTHENING under load (eccentric action) is producing a
braking/decelerating force, and the resulting joint motion is in the OPPOSITE direction to what
that muscle would produce if it were shortening (concentric) at the same activation. Eccentric
contractions can also produce more force than concentric contractions for a given level of
neural drive/EMG — "people can produce large torques with lower surface EMG during eccentric
contractions" — Paschalis, Margaritelis, Chatzinikolaou, Theodorou & Nikolaidis, "Eccentric
Exercise and Muscle Damage: An Introductory Guide," *J Funct Morphol Kinesiol* 2026. PMID
42029507, PMCID PMC13108084 [S40]. This is the direct mechanistic reason **EMG amplitude alone
cannot distinguish concentric from eccentric action, and therefore cannot alone tell you which
direction a muscle is driving a joint** — the same or even lower activation can produce a larger
eccentric force than an equivalent concentric contraction would.

**Golf-specific, load-bearing example — this project's own resolved pectoralis major finding:**
this project's shoulder research (`docs/research/04-shoulder-arm-forearm-and-grip.md` §1A/§2.10,
sourced from McHardy A, Pollard H (2005), "Muscle Activity During the Golf Swing," *Br J Sports
Med* 39(11):799-804, PMCID PMC1725059, Discussion p.801-802, independently re-verified against
the primary PDF) found trail and lead pectoralis major at the SAME EMG amplitude (93%/93% MMT)
during acceleration, with the primary source's own Discussion explaining this mechanistically:
"the right side continues the activity achieved during the early down swing, while the left
pectoralis appears to maintain an eccentric contraction to control the left arm abduction and
external rotation" — trail pec major contracts CONCENTRICALLY to drive internal
rotation/adduction (the power stroke), lead pec major contracts ECCENTRICALLY at the same
recorded amplitude to brake/control the lead arm. Identical activation, opposite mechanical job,
exactly the case this task flagged as critical. The same source describes the follow-through
rotator cuff (trail external rotators / lead internal rotators, i.e. trail infraspinatus/teres
minor and lead subscapularis) as eccentrically loaded to decelerate the swing, proposed as a
mechanism for golf-related shoulder injury, and reports the wrist flexors undergo a sudden
concentric-to-eccentric switch at impact when the club/ground forces a deceleration event —
proposed mechanism for wrist-flexor overuse injury.

**Independent, non-golf corroboration that deceleration phases of throwing/striking sports rely
on eccentric antagonist action:** Trasolini, Nicholson, Mylott et al. 2022, "Biomechanical
Analysis of the Throwing Athlete and Its Impact on Return to Sport," *Arthroscopy, Sports
Medicine, and Rehabilitation* 4(1). PMID 35141540, PMCID PMC8811517 [S39]: "the shoulder must be
safely decelerated, compressed, and stabilized by forceful contraction of the infraspinatus,
teres minor and major, latissimus dorsi, and posterior deltoid. Elbow extension must also be
decelerated by way of eccentric contraction of the elbow flexors." This independently corroborates
the same pattern McHardy & Pollard describe for golf — deceleration phases are driven by eccentric
contraction of muscles whose concentric action is nominally in the opposite direction to the
observed joint motion.

**Also within this report's own §2:** the golf low-back-pain review [S18] states gluteus medius
acts eccentrically during the backswing (controlling/decelerating pelvic motion) while gluteus
maximus stabilises the pelvis — a further concrete golf-specific eccentric example outside the
shoulder girdle.

**Consequence for the app:** any muscle zone that is "lit up" (high activation colour) cannot be
assumed to be concentrically producing its textbook action — for biarticular AND monoarticular
muscles alike, high activation during a phase where the joint is visibly moving in the OPPOSITE
direction to that muscle's concentric action is the expected, physiologically normal signature of
eccentric braking, not an error in the data or the model.

## 6. Force-length and force-velocity relationships

**Force-velocity:** muscle force output is not a fixed function of activation — it depends
strongly on the muscle's instantaneous shortening/lengthening velocity. On the concentric
(shortening) side, force falls as shortening velocity rises (classical Hill relationship,
referenced as underpinning "much of our understanding of muscle force generation during dynamic
movements"); on the eccentric (lengthening) side, force RISES above the isometric maximum as
lengthening velocity increases, in a biphasic pattern — "an initial rapid and steep phase-1
response followed by a longer and shallower phase-2 response" before plateauing — Kissane RWP,
Askew GN, "Starting length and temperature dependence of eccentric muscle force-velocity
behaviour," *J Exp Biol* 2026. PMID 42108769, PMCID PMC13286365 [S41]. The paper explicitly notes
force depends on "the point during the ramp at which force is measured" — i.e. velocity and
timing, not activation, are first-order determinants of instantaneous force during a fast
movement such as a golf swing.

**Force-length:** starting sarcomere/muscle length materially changes the RATE and magnitude of
force development. On the ascending limb (shorter-than-optimal starting lengths), fewer
cross-bridges are attached and force development is measurably slower: rate of relative force
development at 0.95 L₀ (near optimal length) was significantly higher than at 0.90 L₀ (P=0.0012)
and 0.85 L₀ (P<0.001) — same source [S41]. This means a muscle at identical activation produces
different force depending on how stretched/shortened it already is when the signal arrives — e.g.
a muscle pre-stretched at the top of the backswing (X-factor stretch, [S17]) is at a different
point on its force-length curve than the same muscle at address.

**Direct consequence — activation ≠ force in a fast movement:** combining the force-velocity and
force-length findings with §5's eccentric-force finding [S40] ("people can produce large torques
with lower surface EMG during eccentric contractions"): three independent physiological
mechanisms (velocity, length, contraction mode) each decouple measured/inferred activation from
actual force output. A golf swing is fast (peak trunk rotation velocities and joint angular
velocities are high — see this project's kinematics research, T-032) and involves large,
continuous length changes at almost every joint simultaneously, so all three decoupling
mechanisms are active concurrently throughout the swing. **This is the central limitation of any
EMG/activation-based colour-mapping approach**: the same %MVC/%MMT number can correspond to
substantially different force and motion outputs depending on the phase of the swing, making
activation a necessary but explicitly insufficient input for inferring motion — it must be
combined with joint angle/velocity context (kinematics) and, where available, contraction-mode
inference (§5) to be mechanically meaningful.
