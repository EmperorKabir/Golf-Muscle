# 15 — Contraction States, Force–Velocity, and Motor Control: Verification of the "Fire/Relax, No Direction" Claim

Purpose: rigorously check the owner's claim that (a) a muscle can only "contract or relax," (b) direction of pull is therefore irrelevant to the app, and (c) firing/relaxing the correct muscles at correct times produces the golf swing "automatically, perfectly." Evidence gathered from Europe PMC / PMC full text, sequentially, per the six-point brief.

---

## 1. Is "contract or relax" a complete description of muscle states?

**No — three physiologically distinct contraction modes exist, and eccentric contraction is neurally "firing," not "relaxing."**

- Skeletal muscle physiology recognises three contraction types relative to external load: **concentric** (muscle shortens while producing force), **isometric** (force produced, no length change), **eccentric** (muscle is actively lengthened by an external force while still generating force/resisting).
- Eccentric contraction is **not** passive relaxation. It is a distinct active, neurally-driven state:
  - Duchateau J, Enoka RM. "Neural control of lengthening contractions." *J Exp Biol.* 2016;219(Pt 2):197–204. doi:10.1242/jeb.123158. PMID 26792331. — "the control strategy employed by the nervous system during lengthening (eccentric) contractions differs from those used during shortening (concentric) and isometric contractions." The muscle is actively controlled and can be near-maximally activated during lengthening; the review notes **discharge rate is systematically lower** during lengthening than shortening for a given task, and **spinal and corticospinal excitability are reduced** during lengthening vs shortening/isometric — evidence of distinct (not absent) neural drive, i.e. a genuine third control state, not "relax."
  - Untrained individuals often **cannot fully voluntarily activate** muscle during maximal eccentric contraction (same review) — a further sign eccentric control is a separate, harder-to-learn neuromuscular skill, not the default "off" state.
- Titin-based mechanism reviews confirm eccentric contraction is an actively regulated high-force, low-metabolic-cost state, not deactivation:
  - Nishikawa K. "Titin: a tunable spring in active muscle." *Physiology* / related — see also: "Physiological Mechanisms of Eccentric Contraction and Its Applications: A Role for the Giant Titin Protein." *Front Physiol.* 2017. PMCID PMC5299520. PMID 28232805. — titin stiffness increases with activation (Ca²⁺- and force-dependent) during lengthening, explaining eccentric contraction's high force at low metabolic/energetic cost.
  - Herzog W. "Skeletal muscle mechanics: questions, problems and possible solutions." / "Basic Science and Clinical Use of Eccentric Contractions: History and Uncertainties." *J Sport Health Sci.* 2018. PMCID PMC6189250. PMID 30356648. — reviews history; cites Abbott, Bigland & Ritchie (1952) that "maximum muscle force is much greater during eccentric contraction than during shortening contraction," and that comparable metabolic/perceived effort on a linked bicycle produces **100 W concentric ≈ 400–500 W eccentric** output — i.e. the same neural "cost"/activation yields wildly different mechanical output depending on contraction mode.

**Verdict on Point 1: the owner's binary is INCOMPLETE / physiologically inaccurate.** A muscle has (at minimum) three active control states — concentric, isometric, eccentric — plus true relaxation (no activation) as a fourth, distinct, quiescent state. Eccentric action is neural "firing" under lengthening, mechanically and metabolically unlike either concentric firing or relaxation. Any instructional model that only offers "fire" or "relax" cannot represent the eccentric control demand that dominates deceleration phases of a fast movement like a golf swing (e.g., trail-side muscles decelerating the arm/trunk post-impact).

## 2. Force–velocity relationship: eccentric > isometric > concentric at matched activation

**Confirmed, with quantification.**

- Classic force–velocity curve (Hill/Abbott, Bigland & Ritchie 1952, cited in Herzog 2018 PMC6189250): concentric force falls as shortening velocity rises; force rises above isometric maximum as soon as the muscle is lengthened (eccentric side of the curve), then plateaus at a higher force for further lengthening velocity.
- Single-fibre quantification: Lombardi V, Piazzesi G (1990), cited in Herzog 2018 (PMC6189250) — force during active lengthening of isolated frog muscle fibres was **"nearly double the isometric force."**
- Energetic-equivalence data (Abbott, Bigland & Ritchie 1952, via Herzog 2018): on a mechanically linked tandem cycle, a **subjective/metabolic effort level** that produces 100 W concentrically can resist/produce 400–500 W eccentrically — i.e., for comparable neural drive/effort, eccentric mechanical output is roughly 4–5× concentric.
- Residual force enhancement (a related but distinct phenomenon — extra force retained *after* an eccentric stretch, on top of the eccentric-vs-concentric gap): magnitude ~20% at single-fibre level vs ~5% at whole-joint level in humans (Bakenecker et al., *J Sport Health Sci* 2025, PMC11863329, PMID 39454825), and 7–12% at long muscle lengths in human quadriceps (PMC9129877, PMID 35579415, *eLife* 2022).
- Titin-based mechanism (PMC5299520): explains why eccentric force is elevated **without added metabolic/ATP cost** — a passive spring-like element stiffens with Ca²⁺ and force, layering extra force onto the same cross-bridge activation.

**Consequence made explicit by these sources:** identical neural drive/EMG amplitude does **not** imply identical force output — the same "how much" of a contraction signal produces a different force depending on which of the three modes the muscle is in. This directly undercuts an instruction system that specifies only "fire this muscle" without specifying *how* (concentrically, eccentrically, isometrically) — the resulting force, and hence the resulting motion, is indeterminate without that information.

**Verdict on Point 2: CORRECT as stated in the brief, and confirmed quantitatively** (eccentric ≳ isometric > concentric; ~2× at the single-fibre level; ~4–5× at matched-effort whole-body level for the energetic proxy).

## 3. Is a muscle activation pattern (alone) sufficient to determine movement?

**Kinematically yes, in the strict forward-dynamics sense, PROVIDED the activation map is truly complete and is paired with initial conditions, body/segment parameters, and external forces as separate required inputs. It is NOT sufficient from activation timing alone, and anatomical "direction" of a muscle does not predict the resulting joint motion without the rest of the system.**

- **Lombard's paradox** — the textbook demonstration that activation pattern and anatomical classification do not map simply to joint direction:
  - Miyake T, Okabe M. "Roles of Mono- and Bi-articular Muscles in Human Limbs: Two-joint Link Model and Applications." *Integr Org Biol.* 2022. PMCID PMC9681132. PMID 36441608. — "When the rectus femoris contracts... it flexes the hip joint but extends the knee joint. The biceps femoris long head extends the hip joint but flexes the knee joint," yet "during running, both the hip joint and the knee joint extend when one heel touches the ground" — simultaneous co-contraction of anatomically opposing biarticular muscles produces the *same-direction* net joint extension at both joints. Resolution requires moment-arm and net-external-force analysis, not muscle identity: "the realization of an external force of certain direction and magnitude always requires the control of a distinct combination of net moment[s]," and "the actual magnitude of each angle will vary with... different postures and/or different activities" — i.e. the same muscles, activated similarly, produce **different net joint motion at different postures**.
  - Andrews JG. "The Functional Roles of the Hamstrings and Quadriceps During Cycling: Lombard's Paradox Revisited." *J Biomech.* 1987. PMID 3611133. — confirms "considerable paradoxical muscle behavior" across the pedal cycle under two independent analytical methods.
  - Gregor RJ, Cavanagh PR, LaFortune M. "Knee Flexor Moments During Propulsion in Cycling — A Creative Solution to Lombard's Paradox." *J Biomech.* 1985. PMID 4008501.
  - Szecsi J, Straube A, Fornusek C. *J Neuroeng Rehabil.* 2014. PMCID PMC4143553. PMID 25128292. — even in open-loop FES cycling (activation pattern imposed directly, no voluntary motor control confound), "extra concentric work was necessary to overcome considerable eccentric work" — the imposed activation pattern alone did not yield the intended net output without correction, evidencing sensitivity to dynamic/postural context.
  - Watanabe K, Vieira TM, Gallina A, Kouzaki M, Moritani T. "Novel Insights Into Biarticular Muscle Actions Gained From High-Density Electromyogram." *Exerc Sport Sci Rev.* 2021. PMCID PMC8191471. PMID 33927163. — "regional activation of biarticular muscles may contribute to different actions about the joints they span," challenging the assumption that a muscle has one fixed "action" at all — even within one muscle, sub-regions can act differently depending on task.

- **Dependence on posture / initial position / whole-body dynamics ("induced acceleration")**:
  - Piazza SJ et al. "Beyond Inverse Dynamics: Methods for Assessment of Individual Muscle Function during Gait." *Bioengineering.* 2024;11(9):896. doi:10.3390/bioengineering11090896. PMCID PMC11429282. PMID 39329638. — "Joint-level estimates... provide information only about the collective actions of muscles, and not about the roles played by individual muscles." Critically: **"rectus femoris, which passes anterior to the hip and is thus anatomically classified as a hip flexor muscle, has been shown to produce hip extension acceleration during walking"** — a muscle's real-world mechanical effect can be the *opposite* of its anatomical/"direction" classification, because "multi-articular muscles [can] produce accelerations opposite to their anatomical classifications due to the coupled nature of the linked-segment dynamics." The paper also warns that induced-acceleration estimates are "highly sensitive to the choice of... degrees of freedom in the segmented model" — i.e. even the *modeller's* attribution of direction is model-dependent, not a fixed fact about the muscle.
  - Frontiers/J Biomech 2022, "Muscle actions on crossed and non-crossed joints during upright standing and gait" (PMID 34847446) — "particular biarticular muscles presented actions opposite to their anatomical classification for specific joints," and actions were "phase- and task-specific."
  - "Altered Muscle Contributions are Required to Support the Stance Limb During Voluntary Toe-Walking." *Front Bioeng Biotechnol.* 2022. PMCID PMC9036482. PMID 35480978. — identical muscles, different posture (normal walk vs toe-walk) → functionally different joint contributions from the same muscles.

- **Momentum/inertia and interaction torque — movement is driven in part by forces that are not any muscle's instantaneous contraction**:
  - Hirashima M et al. "Multicomponent control strategy underlying production of maximal hand velocity during horizontal arm swing." *J Neurophysiol.* 2009. PMCID PMC2777815. PMID 19759324. — "The trunk provided a crucial contribution, directly, rotating the entire arm, and indirectly, exerting **interaction torque** that caused swift elbow extension," while "the role of the elbow was to maximally comply with passive influence of proximal joints." A distal joint accelerates because of torque *transmitted mechanically* from a proximal segment's motion — not because of any muscle crossing that distal joint contracting at that instant.

**Verdict on Point 3: the owner's core claim is directionally CORRECT but requires two large caveats.** (a) A *sufficiently complete* activation-plus-initial-conditions-plus-external-forces specification does determine the resulting kinematics (this is exactly what forward-dynamics musculoskeletal simulation does — see §Sharpened-question analysis below). (b) But **anatomical "direction"/classification of a muscle does not predict the direction of the resulting joint movement** — Lombard's paradox and induced-acceleration studies show the same muscle, similarly activated, can produce the *opposite* net joint action depending on posture, co-activation, and whole-body dynamics. This actually *supports* the owner's instinct that per-muscle "direction" is not the right unit of information — but it lands the emphasis differently than the owner intended: it is not that direction is irrelevant because only contraction/relaxation matters; it is that *anatomical* direction is an unreliable predictor of *net* movement direction, which is why the modeller needs the whole coupled system, and the user does not need "direction" framed per-muscle at all (see Point 6).

## 4. Motor control literature: synergies, not individual muscles

**Confirmed — the CNS does not appear to command individual muscles independently; it commands a low-dimensional set of synergies/modules, and there is direct evidence people cannot volitionally isolate single motor units even with training and biofeedback.**

- Hug F, Avrillon S, Ibáñez J, Farina D. "Common synaptic input, synergies and size principle: control of spinal motor neurons for movement generation." *J Physiol.* 2023. PMCID PMC10098498. PMID 36353890. — motor neurons receive "common synaptic input" that groups them into "functional groups (clusters)." Central finding: **"selective volitional control of single motor neurons within a cluster receiving common inputs cannot be achieved."** Even under experimental biofeedback, "participants... provided with real-time feedback of the activity of pairs of motor neurons... failed to volitionally control individual motor neurons." The paper situates this within Bernstein's (1947) degrees-of-freedom problem — "the possible strategies used by the CNS to reduce the computational burden of co-ordinating several thousand motor neurons across many muscles."
- Monte A et al. "Muscle synergies for multidirectional isometric force generation during maintenance of upright standing posture." *Exp Brain Res.* 2024. PMCID PMC11252224. PMID 38874594. — **"an average of 5–6 muscle synergies were sufficient to account for a wide variety of EMG waveforms"** across changes in force magnitude and direction — i.e. the nervous system's actual output repertoire is low-dimensional, not per-muscle.
- Cheung VC, d'Avella A, Bizzi E. "Adjustments of motor pattern for load compensation via modulated activations of muscle synergies during natural behaviors." *J Neurophysiol.* 2009. PMCID PMC2666413. PMID 19091930. — under changing mechanical load, the nervous system adjusts by **rescaling/recombining existing synergies**, not by independently reprogramming each muscle.
- Borzelli D et al. *Front Neural Circuits.* 2025. PMCID PMC12634596. PMID 41281379. — "motor tasks require the flexible selection and coordination of multiple muscles, which may be achieved through the organization and combination of muscle synergies."
- "Optimization of modularity during development to simplify walking control across multiple steps." *Front Neural Circuits.* 2023. PMCID PMC10853381. PMID 38343616. — synergies exist specifically to "reduce... the number of degrees of freedom that needs to be regulated," and this modularity is itself developmentally tuned/optimised, not innate at full adult efficiency.
- Ting LH, Gick B, Kesar TM, Xu J. "Ethnokinesiology: towards a neuromechanical understanding of cultural differences in movement." *Phil Trans R Soc B.* 2024. PMCID PMC11529631. PMID 39155720. — frames motor control generally as "low-dimensional functional mappings between sensorimotor and ecological task spaces," consistent with the synergy framework across the wider Ting/Bizzi/d'Avella literature.

**Verdict on Point 4: this is a serious problem for the app's instructional premise as literally stated.** If the nervous system's natural control currency is a handful of synergies (~5–6 in the cited postural-control study) rather than dozens of independently addressable muscles, then an instruction to "fire muscle X at time T, independent of muscles Y and Z" is not neurally executable in the way the instruction implies — the athlete cannot dial in one named muscle in isolation; they can only recruit whichever synergy already contains it, along with everything else that synergy contains, at whatever relative weighting that synergy uses. Directly relevant: even with real-time biofeedback training on isolated motor units, subjects still failed. This does not mean the app's underlying anatomical/EMG *model* is wrong — the model can still be built per-muscle for analysis/coaching-cue purposes — but it means "the golfer fires the correct individual muscles, therefore the swing happens automatically" overstates what conscious, voluntary, per-muscle control can achieve. Existing motor programmes (learned synergies) are almost certainly doing more of the real-time work than any explicit per-muscle instruction could.

## 5. Conscious attention to individual muscles: helps or harms fast skilled movement?

**Harms it — this is one of the most robust and directly on-point findings in the whole brief. Internal focus of attention (on one's own body parts/muscles) degrades performance of skilled, fast, ballistic movement relative to external focus (on movement effect/environment), including in golf specifically.**

- Wang et al. *Front Psychol.* 2026. PMCID PMC13199073. PMID 42199276. — **golf putting**, directly on-topic: "all three attentional focus cues significantly improved putting accuracy compared with the control condition" (children study; adult literature below shows the internal/external split more sharply).
- Zang et al. Meta-analysis. *PeerJ.* 2025;13:e20012. doi:10.7717/peerj.20012. PMCID PMC12424610. PMID 40949725. — pooled effect sizes: **distal-external vs internal focus SMD = 0.59** (95% CI 0.14–1.05, p=0.01); **proximal-external vs internal focus SMD = 0.32** (95% CI 0.13–0.51, p=0.001); **distal- vs proximal-external SMD = 0.30** (95% CI 0.07–0.53, p=0.01). States "focus on one's own movements disrupts the coordination of the motor system, impairing automatic control processes and leading to movement dysregulation," and that this effect is **stronger in experienced performers** than novices ("no significant difference... in novices" between distal/proximal external focus, but a clear internal-focus penalty in skilled performers). This is the **constrained action hypothesis** (Wulf, McNevin & Shea, 2001, as reflected in this literature): internal focus forces conscious, explicit control over movement components that a trained motor system otherwise runs automatically/reflexively, and that intrusion of conscious control is what degrades speed/accuracy/efficiency — i.e. exactly the opposite of what the app's premise (consciously firing specific muscles) would require the golfer to do during the swing itself.
- Luo et al. *Front Psychol.* 2025. PMCID PMC12517065. PMID 41089649. — **basketball shooting**: accuracy 75.0% (external) vs 65.0% (internal); under fatigue, external focus gave **15 percentage points higher accuracy** than internal.
- Niźnikowski et al. *Sports.* 2025. PMCID PMC12568229. PMID 41150467. — **soccer penalty kicks**: external-focus-plus-task-constraint group significantly outperformed internal-focus conditions.
- Daou et al. *Front Psychol.* 2026. PMCID PMC13044087. PMID 41937828. — external focus reduced perceived exertion and *increased* time-to-failure in a fatiguing isometric task versus internal focus.
- Abdi et al. *Sci Rep.* 2025. PMCID PMC12521493. PMID 41087502. — external focus advantage holds especially at lower task difficulty; under higher complexity a "holistic" (whole-movement) focus sustains performance better than either pure internal or pure external.
- Lola et al. *Children.* 2024. PMCID PMC11727623. PMID 39767989. — volleyball: external-focus group showed greater gains in both movement form and outcome versus internal focus.
- Grain et al. *Eur J Neurosci.* 2026. PMCID PMC13249535. PMID 42264551. — neural corroboration: internal focus and divided attention degraded gait performance, and this "induced more conscious control interfering with automatic control processes" (elevated prefrontal engagement accompanying the impairment).

**Verdict on Point 5: this is direct evidence against using the app's muscle-by-muscle model as an in-the-moment execution cue for a fast ballistic skill.** A golf swing (roughly 1–1.5 seconds backswing-to-finish, with the downswing itself under ~0.3 s) is exactly the class of fast, skilled, ballistic movement in which internal focus has repeatedly been shown to degrade performance, especially in more skilled performers — the population the app is presumably most useful to as a coaching tool. This does not invalidate the app's analytical/anatomical model; it constrains **how** that model should be delivered to the user (e.g. as external-focus-compatible cues, or as post-hoc/practice-phase feedback rather than a live "fire this muscle now" prompt during the swing itself).

## 6. Is muscle-pull direction needed by the user, or only by the modeller?

**Needed by the modeller; not needed by the user — with the important refinement (from Point 3) that even the modeller cannot rely on a muscle's *anatomical* direction/classification alone.**

- The modeller needs *some* directional/moment-arm information to convert a muscle's activation into its contribution to net joint torque — this is the entire machinery of inverse/forward dynamics and induced-acceleration analysis (Piazza et al. 2024, PMC11429282; Xie et al. 2026, PMC12977398). Musculotendon length and moment arm are computed **as a function of joint angle** (Xie et al., Eq. 8 in "Muscle contraction dynamics"), which is itself an emergent output of the whole-body dynamics, not a fixed per-muscle fact.
- But per Point 3, the *anatomical* direction/classification (e.g. "rectus femoris is a hip flexor") is frequently **not** what the muscle actually does moment-to-moment in a real, coupled, multi-joint movement (Piazza et al. 2024: rectus femoris "produce[s] hip extension acceleration during walking" despite being anatomically a flexor). So even the modeller cannot use a static "this muscle pulls in direction D" label safely — direction must be computed dynamically from the whole system (posture + moment arms + external forces + neighbouring segment motion), not looked up from anatomy once.
- For the **user**: none of the literature reviewed supports giving a golfer a per-muscle "direction" instruction. The user-facing instruction that is physiologically meaningful is activation timing/intensity (what to contract, when, how hard) — exactly the owner's model — *not* which direction that contraction pulls, because (a) the muscle itself has no "direction" state to set independent of contracting (Point 1 nuance aside — even eccentric/concentric is a resultant, not a directly commanded state, see the sharpened-question analysis below), and (b) even if it did, per Point 3 the net effect of that pull on club/body direction is not a fixed, learnable one-to-one fact but a function of the whole kinetic chain at that instant.

**Verdict on Point 6: CORRECT.** Direction is a modelling/data-interpretation construct (needed to build and validate the underlying biomechanical model correctly, and to avoid mis-attributing a muscle's contribution to the swing), not something that should or could be surfaced to the user as a discrete instruction.

## Sharpened question (raised by project owner): "Stretching is itself caused by some muscle contracting somewhere in the chain — so eccentric/concentric shouldn't matter, since a complete activation map already determines it?"

This is a materially different and more precise claim than the original brief, and deserves a direct, undiluted verdict.

### 7.1 — Is contraction mode fully determined by a complete activation map + fixed initial conditions?

**Yes, in the strict forward-dynamics sense — this part of the owner's argument is CORRECT, and is not a marginal or contestable point; it is how forward-dynamics musculoskeletal simulation is defined.**

- Xie H et al. "A forward dynamics framework for parameter optimization of the EMG-driven musculoskeletal model." *J Neuroeng Rehabil.* 2026;23:91. doi:10.1186/s12984-025-01752-3. PMCID PMC12977398. PMID 41654909. — the standard EMG-driven pipeline: activation dynamics (EMG → activation) → muscle contraction dynamics, in which **musculotendon length and moment arm are computed from joint angle** (their Eq. 8) → joint moment → (in the forward-dynamics direction) integration of the equations of motion to update joint angle/velocity over time. Muscle length trajectory — and therefore whether the muscle is momentarily shortening, lengthening, or static — is **read off the joint-angle trajectory**, which is itself the *output* of integrating activation-derived moments together with the body's inertial properties and any external forces, starting from specified initial position and velocity. This is precisely a deterministic forward-dynamics system: same activation history + same initial state + same body/environment model → same kinematics → same per-muscle contraction-mode history, every time.
- This is uncontroversial biomechanics/control-theory: it is exactly what packages such as OpenSim's forward-dynamics tool do, and it is the premise on which the entire EMG-driven-simulation literature operates.

**So: yes — contraction mode (concentric/eccentric/isometric) is not an independent causal input alongside activation. It is an emergent, computable output of activation + initial conditions + body dynamics + external forces.** This is a genuine concession the research supports.

### 7.2 — But "activation map" alone is not the complete input set. What else is required, and what causes lengthening that is not "another muscle contracting right now"?

Four separate non-activation inputs are required, and all four matter for a golf swing:

**(a) Initial conditions — specifically velocity/momentum, not just position.** The forward-dynamics equations require initial joint angle **and** initial joint angular velocity for every degree of freedom. Momentum built up earlier in the swing is carried forward as stored kinetic energy that is *not* re-specified by any muscle's current activation level — it is a state variable, not a control signal.
- Hirashima M et al. *J Neurophysiol.* 2009. PMCID PMC2777815. PMID 19759324. — direct empirical demonstration in an arm-swing task: elbow extension was driven by **"interaction torque"** transmitted from trunk rotation, and "the role of the elbow was to maximally comply with passive influence of proximal joints" — i.e. the elbow (and the muscles crossing it) moved because of momentum/torque transferred mechanically from a *different, already-completed* segment rotation, not because any muscle crossing the elbow was contracting to cause that motion at that instant.
- **In-project corroboration, golf-specific and already independently verified** (`docs/research/04-shoulder-arm-forearm-and-grip.md`, drawing on Pink, Jobe & Perry 1990 EMG data, verified against primary sources per this project's own commit history): trail subscapularis and lead infraspinatus both undergo a **"concentric-to-eccentric switch"** within the first fraction of a second after impact — trail subscapularis 64% MVC and lead infraspinatus 61% MVC at early follow-through — explicitly interpreted as the rotator cuff **eccentrically decelerating** angular velocity that was generated earlier, during the acceleration/downswing phase, by different muscles that are no longer contracting at that later instant. The stretch these decelerator muscles undergo is driven by the arm/club's momentum, not by a currently-active antagonist. Critically, this is not passive drift: the decelerators are *themselves highly active* (60%+ MVC) — this is Duchateau & Enoka's "genuine third state" from Point 1, actively resisting momentum, not "relaxing" and being dragged.
- Conclusion: momentum is *historically* attributable to earlier muscle contraction (kinetic energy had to be put in by something), but at the instant it is causing a given muscle to lengthen, it is **temporally decoupled** from any concurrently-contracting muscle. The owner's phrase "other muscles... causing that muscle to stretch" is true only if read to include muscles that contracted *earlier and are no longer contracting* — which is a materially different, and instructionally important, situation from a live antagonist pull.

**(b) Gravity.** A constant external force, wholly independent of any muscle's activation state at any instant. It always contributes to the equations of motion and therefore to whether a given muscle is lengthening or shortening at a given moment; it is never "caused" by a muscle contracting.

**(c) Ground reaction force / club–ball contact.** External contact forces transmitted through the kinetic chain; partly reactive to the golfer's own muscular effort (e.g., leg drive against the ground) but not reducible to "a muscle contracting," since it also reflects body weight, ground stiffness, and — after impact — the ball/club collision, none of which is a muscle event.

**(d) Passive elastic tissue — force at zero activation.** Tendon, ligament, joint capsule, fascia, and passive (non-cross-bridge) muscle stiffness at long length all generate real mechanical force/energy with **no concurrent muscle activation** at all:
- Schroeder BJ, Kuo AD. "Elastic energy savings and active energy cost in a simple model of running." *PLoS Comput Biol.* 2021. PMCID PMC8651147. PMID 34813593. — in a running model, **59% of positive work** was returned through passive elastic recoil rather than generated by active (concentric) muscle shortening.
- "Non-Cross Bridge Viscoelastic Elements Contribute to Muscle Force and Work During Stretch-Shortening Cycles." *Front Physiol.* 2021. PMCID PMC8039322. PMID 33854441. — passive viscoelastic elements measurably add force/work on top of (i.e., independent of) cross-bridge/activation-driven force.
- Front Physiol 2017 (titin review, PMC5299520/PMID 28232805) — titin's Ca²⁺-/force-dependent stiffening adds force during lengthening at essentially no added metabolic (i.e. activation) cost — force rising without a proportional rise in "how much the muscle is firing."
- Esposito et al. *Sci Rep.* 2022. PMCID PMC8907303. PMID 35264630. — quantifies Achilles tendon elastic energy return magnitude (~3.9% of per-step mechanical energy attributable to a footwear-mediated stretch change) as one measurable slice of a larger passive-elastic energy budget.

**7.2 verdict: the owner's premise ("a complete activation map... determines contraction mode") is true only when "activation map" is silently bundled with (i) full initial position *and* velocity for every DOF, (ii) gravity, (iii) ground/club contact forces, and (iv) passive tissue properties. None of (i)–(iv) is itself an "activation" of a muscle. So the strong reading of the owner's claim — that activation timing alone, with nothing else specified, determines contraction mode — is INCORRECT. The weaker, defensible reading — that contraction mode is an emergent physical consequence of activation *together with* the rest of body dynamics, and is not a separate thing the CNS or the app needs to command *in addition to* activation — is CORRECT.**

### 7.3 — Does eccentric/concentric carry information beyond an activation+time map? Two separate answers.

**(i) Instruction model (what the golfer is told to do): the owner's extension is essentially CORRECT, and is the strongest form of their overall thesis.** If the goal is telling the golfer *what to do*, "contract muscle X to intensity Y at time T" is a complete and sufficient instruction — the app does not additionally need to tell the golfer "...and this will be eccentric" any more than it needs to tell them "...and this will pull in direction D" (Point 6). Whether that contraction ends up concentric, eccentric, or isometric is exactly as automatic, physics-determined, and non-instructable as direction is. This is a genuine, non-trivial concession: **the same logic the owner originally applied to direction extends cleanly to contraction mode, and the research supports that extension.**
 - The real limitation is not physics-level (contraction mode is not a fireable, separate control input) — it is **achievability**, from Points 4–5: a human cannot in fact specify or execute a complete, muscle-by-muscle, millisecond-precise activation map as a voluntary act (Hug et al. 2023, PMC10098498: "selective volitional control of single motor neurons within a cluster receiving common inputs cannot be achieved," even with biofeedback training). And consciously attending to individual muscles during a fast skilled action degrades performance (Point 5, Zang et al. 2025 meta-analysis, PMC12424610). So the owner is correct that contraction mode need not be separately instructed — but this is because *no* individual-muscle instruction is a viable live-execution model in the first place, not because per-muscle activation instructions are otherwise sound. The "automatic, perfect" swing the owner describes is real, but it is produced by trained motor synergies executing a learned pattern, not by the golfer consciously sequencing named muscles from an app in real time.

**(ii) Data-validation model (checking a published activation value against observed motion): the eccentric/concentric label is NOT redundant with the activation number, and should NOT be discarded.** Point 2's force–velocity data show the same activation/EMG level produces different force depending on contraction mode (eccentric force ≈ up to ~2× isometric at the single-fibre level, Lombardi & Piazzesi 1990 via Herzog 2018 PMC6189250; comparable-effort eccentric-vs-concentric power output differing roughly 4–5× in the classic linked-cycle data, Abbott, Bigland & Ritchie 1952 via the same review). A published "%MVC" or "%EMGmax" figure does not, by itself, carry the muscle's instantaneous velocity — the modeller is not given the full kinematic state that would let contraction mode be recomputed from first principles; the contraction-mode label *is* the compact proxy for that missing velocity information. Discarding it and treating activation level as force-equivalent across modes would systematically misestimate force/torque, particularly in follow-through-type phases where the literature (McHardy interpretation, in-project) explicitly flags a concentric-to-eccentric switch. This directly matters for this project's own unit-reconciliation tasks (TASKS.md T-019/T-043, reconciling %MMT/%MVC/%EMGmax/raw µV): contraction-mode metadata must be retained per data point, not treated as redundant with the activation number.

### 7.4 — Does the force–velocity relationship matter if the app displays activation/exertion, not force?

**Partially moot for the end-user display layer, but still load-bearing for the modeller/pipeline — these are genuinely separable, and the owner is right to separate them.**

- If the app's user-facing unit is activation/exertion (e.g. "contract this muscle to ~70%"), then, per 7.3(i), the resulting force is automatically whatever muscle physiology produces at that activation level and that instantaneous velocity — the user does not need to be told the multiplier, exactly as they do not need to be told direction. On this point the owner's "activation/exertion, not force" framing is coherent and internally consistent, and the force–velocity relationship does not create an extra instruction the user needs.
- It still matters upstream: wherever the app's activation/exertion numbers are *derived* from published EMG or from inverse-dynamics-estimated joint torque (rather than measured directly as raw EMG), contraction mode must be known to convert correctly between torque and activation (see 7.3(ii)) — an error here would corrupt the activation values the user is shown, even though the user never sees "force" or "eccentric/concentric" as a label. This is a data-pipeline correctness issue, not a UI/instruction issue.

### 7.5 — Direct verdict on the sharpened question

- **Where the owner is right:** contraction mode (eccentric/concentric/isometric) is not an independent thing that needs to be separately commanded on top of activation — it is a deterministic, emergent consequence of activation combined with the rest of body dynamics, exactly analogous to how direction is emergent (Point 6). For the *instruction* the app gives a golfer, this means the app does not need a "tell the user this will be eccentric" feature any more than it needs a "tell the user this pulls left" feature. This is a correct and fairly narrow technical point, and it holds without needing to be softened.
- **Where the owner overstates it:** "a complete activation map... already determines it" quietly assumes initial velocity/momentum, gravity, ground/club contact forces, and passive tissue properties are also given — none of which are muscle activations, and momentum in particular is generated by muscles that are *no longer contracting* by the time the stretch they set in motion occurs (Hirashima 2009; in-project rotator-cuff follow-through data). So "stretching is always just some muscle contracting somewhere" is true only in a diffuse historical-causation sense, not in the sense of a live antagonist doing the pulling — and for a fast, momentum-dominated, single-attempt ballistic motion like a golf downswing/follow-through, that distinction is exactly where control is hardest and most injury-relevant (the decelerators must be actively, eccentrically loaded to *control* momentum they did not create).
- **The remaining, narrow exception that survives:** the eccentric/concentric label must be **retained in the app's internal data model** (for correctly interpreting/validating published EMG-to-force relationships, per 7.3(ii)) even though it need **not** be surfaced as a separate instruction to the user (per 7.3(i)). That is a data-layer requirement, not a UI/instruction-model requirement — which is consistent with, not contradictory to, the owner's original "direction is not relevant to the user" thesis, once "direction" is read as "a per-muscle instruction unit" rather than "information the model needs internally."
