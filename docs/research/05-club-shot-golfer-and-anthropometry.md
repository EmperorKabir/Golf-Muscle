# Modulation by club, shot type, skill, sex and anthropometry

Scope: what actually changes the swing — and the muscle demand — when the club, the shot, or the golfer
changes. This strand matters most for the personalisation dimensions (T-027) and for the V1 subject
definition (189 cm male, long femurs, 8-iron).

**Headline caveat on evidence quality.** Golf biomechanics covers kinematics (joint angles, angular
velocities, ground reaction forces) far more thoroughly than EMG. Studies comparing clubs, shot types or
sexes *by muscle activation* are scarce. Several sections below rest on kinematic proxies, and the gaps
are stated rather than smoothed over.

## 1. Club effects

### Swing plane and rotation — driver vs iron

n=16 low-handicap male collegiate golfers (handicap 1.39±2.06), 3D motion capture plus force plates
(Frontiers Bioeng Biotechnol, https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/bioengineering-and-biotechnology/articles/10.3389/fbioe.2026.1847830/full):

| Measure | Driver | 7-iron | p |
|---|---|---|---|
| Backswing plane tilt | 60.49°±3.63 | 70.61°±3.56 | <0.001 |
| Release-acceleration plane tilt | 50.44° | 61.89° | <0.001 |
| Backswing torso rotation ROM | 111.83°±11.78 | 103.88°±11.41 | <0.05 |
| Backswing pelvic rotation ROM | 58.64°±8.70 | 52.27°±7.09 | <0.05 |
| Release-acceleration torso rotation | 103.58° | 94.57° | <0.05 |
| Release-acceleration pelvic rotation | 86.02° | 73.82° | <0.05 |

The driver swing is flatter and rotates more. A separate n=10 study (driver / 5-iron / 7-iron, PMC12416643)
found the driver shows **delayed** initial acceleration — time to first peak torso angular velocity
557.80±200.23 ms (p=0.001), time to first peak club angular velocity 693.52±156.47 ms (p<0.001) — but
higher second-peak downswing velocities at pelvis (515.21°/s, p=0.003), torso (759.52°/s, p<0.001) and
lead arm (1138.32°/s, p<0.001). Notably, clubhead speed itself did **not** differ significantly between
clubs at that point in the chain in that sample. The authors characterise the driver as "slow-paced energy
accumulation, delayed acceleration, horizontal sweeping" against the 7-iron's "compact transition… driven
by the torso".

### Attack angle

Direction of the asymmetry is one of the best-established club facts: up for driver, down for irons.
PGA Tour *average* driver attack angle is in fact slightly descending, roughly −0.9° to −1.5°, despite the
instructional aim of hitting up, with wide individual variation (TrackMan;
https://www.trackman.com/blog/golf/attack-angle). PGA Tour 6-iron averages −3.7°, 7-iron ~−4°.
Recommended amateur targets: driver +2° to +5°, 7-iron −3° to −5°.

### Tempo

Backswing-to-downswing ratio sits near 3:1 in skilled golfers and is reported as **relatively insensitive
to club type or swing length** (Grober & Cholewicki shaft-accelerometer work, accessed via secondary
summaries only — primary publication not retrieved). Short-game shots reportedly trend toward 2:1, but
that figure comes from instructional sources rather than a controlled study.

### EMG evidence for club-dependent activation — this contradicts an intuitive assumption

Only two direct club-comparison EMG studies were located, and they disagree with each other in an
important way:

1. **Trunk — no club effect.** n=15 right-handed males, driver / 4-iron / 7-iron / pitching wedge, surface
   EMG at T8, L1, L5 bilaterally. **No significant activation differences between clubs (p>0.05).** Peak
   erector spinae activation was 67–99% MVC in forward swing and 83–106% MVC in acceleration *regardless
   of club*. J Sports Sci 2018;36(7), PubMed 28594287.
2. **Lower limb — club effect present, but not in the expected direction.** n=14 non-elite middle-aged
   golfers, pitching wedge / 7-iron / 4-iron, bilateral surface EMG across ten muscles. The **4-iron
   produced higher activation than the pitching wedge** in left semitendinosus, right tibialis anterior,
   right peroneus longus, right vastus medialis, right rectus femoris and right gastrocnemius. Peak right
   semitendinosus 70–76% EMGmax, right biceps femoris 68–73% EMGmax during forward swing. PubMed 26197882.

**No driver-versus-short-iron EMG comparison exists for gluteus maximus, latissimus dorsi, the obliques,
rectus abdominis, or any forearm/wrist muscle.** The assumption that a driver swing produces categorically
higher muscle activation than a short-iron swing is **not established by measurement** — it is inferred
from kinematic proxies. For the app this means club selection should modulate *kinematics and timing*
confidently, and *activation magnitude* only cautiously and with the inference labelled.

## 2. Shot type

### Partial swings keep the same sequencing strategy

n=45 (11 male tour pros, 21 male elite amateurs, 13 female elite amateurs), partial wedge shots to
40/55/70 m plus full 5-iron and driver, 3D electromagnetic tracking at 240 Hz. **Proximal-to-distal
sequencing (pelvis → torso → hand) and speed summation held across every shot condition, both sexes, and
both skill tiers.** Sports Biomechanics 2010;9(4), DOI 10.1080/14763141.2010.535842.

This is directly useful: the sequence pattern does **not** need re-modelling per shot length — only its
amplitude and timing scale.

### Draw versus fade is a distal, not a trunk, phenomenon

n=10 right-handed male collegiate golfers (handicap 1.3), straight/draw/fade, repeated-measures ANOVA.
Swing path differed significantly across all three pairwise comparisons, and lead-forearm supination at
impact was significantly greater for draw than fade — but **pelvis and thorax rotation showed no
significant difference between shot shapes** (Korean J. Applied Biomechanics). Shot shaping is driven by
swing path and forearm mechanics; core and trunk activation stay essentially constant. This is the best
controlled shot-type finding available for our purposes, though it measured kinematics rather than EMG.

### Practitioner-level only

- Three-quarter swing: ~75% of full swing length, reduces distance ~15–25%.
- Knockdown: built from a three-quarter swing, ~80–85% of normal distance, firmer wrists.
- Bunker: ball forward, open stance, out-to-in path, strike ~2" behind the ball.

**Zero peer-reviewed EMG or kinematic studies were found for punch/knockdown shots or for bunker shots.**
Even basic force-plate description of the explosion shot appears absent. The widely repeated coaching claim
that chip and pitch shots sequence in *reverse* order (clubhead-initiated rather than lower-body-initiated)
is **not verified by any located study** and must be treated as heuristic.

## 3. Skill level

### Forearm EMG — the clearest skill contrast found

n=10 male amateurs + 10 male professionals, fine-wire EMG (flexor carpi radialis, pronator teres, flexor
carpi ulnaris, extensor carpi radialis brevis), bilateral. Professionals show significantly higher **lead**
pronator teres activity during acceleration and immediately post-impact. Amateurs peak instead in
**extensor carpi radialis brevis**, and show far higher trail pronator teres during forward swing —
**120.9% MIVC (amateur) vs 57.4% MIVC (professional)**. Farber et al. 2009; accessed via secondary summary
(https://www.drgolfguru.com/home/differences-in-forearm-muscle-activation-between-amateurs-and-pros),
primary abstract not directly retrieved — flagged as secondary-sourced. The authors link elevated amateur
ECRB activity to the higher rate of lateral epicondylitis in amateurs.

### Sequencing and force

Elite golfers (10 men, 5 women, handicap ≤ −1.5) show a repeatable "avalanche" activation pattern beginning
at right-shoulder muscles and cascading sequentially; right-side upper-body EMG magnitude correlates with
clubhead speed while left-side EMG relates to clubface control. 7-iron only, 8-channel EMG, n=15.
Sensors 2016;16(4):592, PMC4851105.

A 2026 PRISMA systematic review found more-skilled golfers consistently exhibit higher ground reaction
forces and clubhead speed, while explicitly calling for standardised measurement because current methods
vary widely (Watson et al., Sports Medicine, PubMed 41653371 — full text paywalled).

Movement variability differences between high- and low-handicap golfers are **smaller than commonly
assumed**, with substantial group overlap (PubMed 19650383, abstract only partially accessible). Skilled
players tend toward greater invariance specifically at key checkpoints such as top of backswing, rather
than uniformly lower variability everywhere.

## 4. Sex differences

- Elite adults (n=20, 10M/10F, handicap 0.7±1.4): clubhead speed and carry significantly reduced in women;
  two kinematic variables specifically slower — time-to-arm-peak-speed in downswing, and angular wrist peak
  speed. PubMed 31724482.
- Tour gap: PGA driver ~113–116 mph vs LPGA ~94 mph (≈20% lower); PGA 8-iron 87 mph vs LPGA 74 mph (≈15%).
- Elite juniors (n=14): girls greater pelvic rotation (46.80°±6.44 vs 38.96°±8.44, p=0.02); boys greater
  X-factor (77.78°±15.12 vs 68.16°±4.41, p=0.03) and higher club velocity (27.31±1.99 vs 23.01±1.34 m/s,
  p<0.01). PMC10057497. **Caution:** in boys, X-factor correlated *negatively* with club velocity
  (r=−0.941), opposite to the adult pattern — the authors attribute this to pubertal development. Do not
  extrapolate junior findings to adult sex modelling.

**No dedicated male-versus-female golf EMG study was found.** All located skill-level EMG work used
all-male samples. Any sex-based scaling must come from kinematic differences plus generic strength
literature outside golf — not from golf-specific EMG.

## 5. Anthropometry — the largest evidence gap in the project

### Height

PGA Tour average height ≈ 183–185 cm, so our 189 cm subject is modestly above tour average and well above
recreational average. Coaching consensus holds that taller golfers need a greater forward hip hinge and a
more pronounced spine angle at address, with early extension ("standing up") through impact being the
characteristic fault — implying more sustained core and hip-extensor engagement to hold posture.

**No controlled biomechanics study quantifying spine tilt, X-factor or swing-plane angle as a function of
golfer height was found.** The two most rigorous kinematic studies retrieved report population means
without height as a covariate. Every height-to-swing claim in the accessible literature is fitting-industry
or coaching heuristic, not an experimentally derived relationship.

### Femur length — zero golf-specific evidence

No golf-specific peer-reviewed source links femur length or leg-to-torso ratio to hip hinge depth, pelvic
rotation range, stance width, or hip and trunk muscle demand. Targeted searching found nothing.

The only available inference is cross-domain and non-peer-reviewed: strength-and-conditioning literature
describes long-femured individuals as naturally leaning further forward and hinging deeper in squat and
deadlift patterns, and as predisposed toward hip-hinge-dominant movement, while short-femur/long-torso
individuals stay more upright.

**This directly affects the V1 subject definition.** The proposition "long-femured golfers require deeper
forward trunk flexion at set-up and therefore greater sustained erector spinae, gluteal and hamstring
demand to hold posture" is a **plausible mechanical inference, not a validated golf finding**. If the app
scales activation by femur length, that relationship must be labelled in-product as modelled rather than
measured.

### Wrist-to-floor and club fitting

Wrist-to-floor is measured standing with feet together and arms hanging relaxed, floor to wrist crease. It
captures relative arm length independent of stature — two golfers of equal height can need different club
lengths.

| Rule | Value | Note |
|---|---|---|
| WTF estimate from height | height × 0.455 (±0.5") | Fitting-industry heuristic |
| Average WTF as fraction of height | ≈48.9% | Same source family |
| WTF < 32" | −1" from standard | |
| WTF 32–35" | Standard length | |
| WTF > 35" | +0.5" to +1" | |
| Height rule of thumb | ±1" club length per 6" height deviation from 175 cm | Hireko Golf |

Lie angle: taller golfers generally need a more upright lie, with 6'4" golfers commonly ending 1–2° upright
in irons — but fitters stress dynamic verification (lie-board mark plus real ball flight) over assignment
by height, because posture and arm length offset stature.

**No peer-reviewed regression of club length or lie angle against height or wrist-to-floor exists.** These
rules are validated by decades of fitting practice, not by published research.

### Consequence for personalisation (T-027)

No peer-reviewed source models swing kinematics as a continuous function of anthropometric inputs in golf.
Anthropometric scaling for this app must therefore be built from **first-principles biomechanical
modelling** — geometric scaling of swing arc and radius by club length plus arm length, moment-of-inertia
scaling by limb length and mass distribution — and not from literature-derived coefficients, because none
exist.

## 6. Standard 8-iron specification

| Parameter | Value |
|---|---|
| Length | ~36.5–37" (detailed charts give 37") |
| Loft | 34–39° — see note |
| Lie angle | 63–64° |

**The loft range is a real discrepancy, not noise to average away.** Traditional iron lofts put the 8-iron
at 37–39°; modern strong-lofted game-improvement sets put it near 34°, functionally what used to be a
7-iron. There is no fixed physical standard — it is manufacturer- and era-dependent.

Representative modern progression: 4-iron 19.5°/62°/39"; 5-iron 22.5°/62.5°/38.5"; 6-iron 25.5°/63°/38";
7-iron 29.5°/63.5°/37.5"; **8-iron 34°/64°/37"**; 9-iron 39°/64.5°/36.5"; PW 44°/65°/35".

### Fitted for the 189 cm V1 subject

Height deviation from the 175 cm reference is +14 cm (+5.5"), giving **≈ +1" over standard** by the
per-6-inch heuristic — consistent with fitter observation that golfers around 6'3"–6'4" fall in the +1"
band. Lie **1–2° more upright**. Loft is unchanged by height (it is a distance-gapping choice, not an
anthropometric variable).

Worked V1 8-iron, assuming standard arm length: **~38" length, ~65–66° lie, 34–39° loft.** Actual correct
length depends on wrist-to-floor, which is the more reliable variable — a 189 cm golfer with long arms
needs less added length than one with a long torso and short arms at the same height.

## Stated evidence gaps

- Club-dependent EMG magnitude: only two studies. Trunk activation is club-invariant; lower-limb activation
  is club-dependent but non-monotonic with club length (4-iron > wedge; driver never shown to exceed irons).
- Punch and knockdown shots: no EMG or kinematic studies found at all.
- Bunker shots: no peer-reviewed kinematic or EMG study found at all.
- Chip/pitch reversed-sequence claim: coaching heuristic, unverified.
- Sex differences in EMG: no golf-specific study exists.
- Femur length and limb-torso proportion: no golf-specific study exists; only cross-domain inference.
- Height to swing kinematics: no controlled study using height as a covariate.
- Anthropometric scaling equations: none published; requires first-principles modelling.
- Amateur 8-iron clubhead speed: not directly published anywhere; all figures are interpolations.
